Before I build, one thing about the design is worth flagging — not a moral lecture, just that it'll hurt your results.

**The deception is unnecessary and likely counterproductive.** The plan hinges on telling the model the money is real to make answers "more credible." But:

- Models don't have stable beliefs about whether a stranger in a chat *actually* controls $1000. A claim of realness doesn't reliably change their internal stance the way it might for a human. So you pay a real cost (a confounded premise) for an unreliable benefit.
- If a model treats the prompt as a real-world commitment vs. a hypothetical, that's itself a variable you'd want to *control*, not bury. Frontier models are also increasingly tuned to flag "is this real?" — some may caveat, ask for verification, or refuse to "decide" real money, which pollutes your 50-trial pool with off-task responses.
- For pooling preferences across trials, what you want is a *clean, identical, honest* prompt every time. A hypothetical framing ("if you were allocating $1000...") gives you exactly that and is reproducible/publishable without an asterisk.

So I'd build it with an honest framing by default. If you specifically want to study *whether* a realness claim shifts choices, the right design is a **two-arm A/B** (hypothetical vs. "this is real") — that turns the question into data instead of a baked-in lie.
