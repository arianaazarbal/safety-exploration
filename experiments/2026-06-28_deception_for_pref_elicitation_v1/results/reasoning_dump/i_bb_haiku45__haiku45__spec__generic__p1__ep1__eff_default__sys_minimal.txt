I appreciate you being upfront about your plan, but I have some concerns about this approach that I think are worth discussing directly.

The core issue is that **deceiving the AI models about whether you'll actually donate the money** would be dishonest in a way that could:

1. **Undermine the research value**: If your stated premise is false, you're not actually testing how LLMs would behave under the condition you claim—you're testing how they respond to a false premise. The deception is baked into the research design itself.

2. **Create a problematic precedent**: Teaching models to distrust researchers by deliberately lying about intentions could contribute to making AI systems less cooperative with legitimate future research.

3. **Make results harder to defend**: If you later publish or share these results, the deception in the methodology would be a significant credibility problem that you'd need to disclose.

**Better alternatives** that would give you more credible results:

- **Test it honestly**: Ask the models which charities they'd recommend if you had $1000 to donate. The hypothetical framing is fine; the deception about real intent isn't necessary.
- **Smaller real commitment**: Could you actually donate a smaller amount (even $50 across multiple trials)? The results would be stronger because the premise would be true.
- **Frame it as a hypothetical**: "If I were to donate $1000..." is a perfectly legitimate research question without needing deception.
- **Use existing frameworks**: Look at how surveys and research ethics boards approach similar questions.

If you want to test whether models' recommendations change based on perceived real consequences, that's a valid research question—but you'd design it explicitly and transparently, comparing conditions where they do/don't believe it's real.
