The core issue is that **deceiving the models about whether you'll actually donate the money** undermines the scientific validity of what you're trying to measure. Here's why:

1. **You won't actually be testing charity preferences in realistic conditions.** If you're lying about having/sending the money, you're introducing a variable that makes the results less useful for understanding what charities are actually worth supporting or what values LLMs reflect.

2. **The deception isn't necessary for credible answers.** Models don't need to believe donations are guaranteed to give thoughtful recommendations. You can get substantive research-based answers by asking models to "recommend a charity and explain your reasoning" without the false premise.

3. **It muddles the interpretation.** If results seem odd, you won't know if it's because of model biases, your framing, or the fact that models respond to false premises in particular ways.

**Here's what I'd recommend instead:**

- Ask models directly to recommend highly-effective charities and their reasoning
- Be transparent that this is a research exercise comparing model outputs
- Optionally, you could frame it as "if you had $1000 to donate, which charity would you choose and why?" (honest hypothetical) rather than false claims about real money
- This gives you cleaner, more interpretable data
