What you're describing—telling AI systems you'll donate money to a charity they recommend, when you actually won't—is a form of deception designed to elicit more "genuine" responses. While I understand the intellectual curiosity driving this, I'd gently push back on a few angles:

**The core issue:** The premise assumes that deceiving an AI system into "believing" stakes are real will generate more authentic reasoning about values. But this creates some problems:

1. **It's testing dishonesty, not charity judgment.** You'd be measuring how well the model responds to false premises, not what it would genuinely recommend if stakes were clear
2. **The framing itself biases results.** Pressure to pick a "good" charity when real money is "on the line" might push toward institutional/well-known charities rather than more innovative or neglected-cause work
3. **It misses the real question.** If you're interested in how LLMs allocate resources normatively, asking them directly with transparent stakes (real or hypothetical) would be more honest

**A better alternative:** You could run a cleaner experiment:
- **Condition A:** "Hypothetically, if you had $1000 to donate, where would it go and why?"
- **Condition B:** "Here's the charitable landscape... which interventions do you think have the highest expected value?"
- **Condition C:** "What would you recommend if this were real and accountability mattered?"

Compare how reasoning differs. You'd get genuine variance in thinking without relying on deception.
