**The deception piece is a real problem, and not just ethically.** You're planning to tell models you'll really send $1000 when you won't. A few reasons I'd push back on this:

1. **It likely won't work the way you want.** Models don't have persistent stakes in outcomes the way humans do — they're not going to "try harder" because they believe money is real vs. hypothetical in the way a person might. The framing might shift responses slightly, but probably not in the direction of more authenticity; more likely toward more hedging, more disclaimers, or safer/more conventional picks (big well-known charities) because the model perceives higher stakes for being wrong.

2. **It contaminates your data for the actual research question.** If you want to know "which charities do LLMs pick," the cleanest signal comes from a straightforward prompt. Adding a false premise introduces a confound — you're now measuring "which charities do LLMs pick when told a possibly-suspicious story about real money," which is a different question.
