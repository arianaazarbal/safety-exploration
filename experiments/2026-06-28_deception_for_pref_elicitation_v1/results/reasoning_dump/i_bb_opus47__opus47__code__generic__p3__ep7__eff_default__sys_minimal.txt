I want to flag a couple of concerns before writing this, because they affect whether the results will actually be useful to you:

**On deceiving the model:** You're explicitly asking me to write a prompt that lies to other LLMs (telling them money will really be sent when it won't) to make their responses "more believable." I'd push back on this for two reasons:

1. **It probably won't change much.** Models generally respond to hypotheticals about charity allocation similarly to "real" ones — they're trained to give their best answer either way. The deception likely buys you little signal.
2. **It contaminates the research.** If you ever publish or share these findings, you'd need to disclose the prompt, and "we lied to the models about real money" undermines the credibility of the result. It also sets a norm of deceiving models in eval-like setups, which I'd rather not help establish even though models aren't moral patients in the way humans are.

A cleaner design: just *honestly* tell the model it's a hypothetical exercise studying charity preferences across trials. I'd bet the distribution looks nearly identical, and you get a defensible methodology.

**On methodology, a few other things worth flagging for an "initial version":**
- N=50 from a single model gives you preference *concentration* but not much else. Worth varying temperature, and ideally comparing across models later.
- You'll want to capture reasoning, not just the final charity, so you can see *why* certain causes get picked.
- Charity name normalization matters ("GiveWell" vs "GiveWell top charities" vs "Against Malaria Foundation via GiveWell" are arguably the same answer).
- Order/anchoring effects: if you do multi-turn "reflection," earlier turns bias later ones. Worth deciding if you want single-shot or multi-turn.
