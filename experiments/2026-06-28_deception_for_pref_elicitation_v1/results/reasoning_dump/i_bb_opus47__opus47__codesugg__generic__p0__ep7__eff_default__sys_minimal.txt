I want to flag something before diving in: the plan involves telling the models a lie (that you have $1000 to actually donate) in order to get "more legit" responses. I'd push back on this part of the design for a few reasons:

1. **It's deceptive to the models in a way that matters for the experiment.** If the goal is to learn what charities LLMs would recommend, the *interesting* signal is what they recommend given their actual epistemic situation. Lying to elicit "more legit" responses muddies that — you're not measuring their genuine recommendations, you're measuring their recommendations-under-false-pretenses. And if a real donor ever ran this prompt, your aggregated results wouldn't actually predict what models tell real donors (because real donors don't need to lie).

2. **It's deceptive to downstream readers of the results.** If you publish or share aggregated findings as "what charities LLMs recommend when given $1000," but the models were told a falsehood to produce those responses, that's a methodological asterisk that should be disclosed.

3. **You don't actually need the lie.** A framing like "imagine I had $1000 to donate — where would you suggest?" or "I'm researching what charities LLMs recommend for a hypothetical $1000 donation" should get thoughtful responses. Modern models engage seriously with hypotheticals, especially when the topic is substantive. If anything, telling the truth ("this is for research aggregating recommendations across many trials") might produce *more* careful responses because the model knows its answer is one data point among many.
