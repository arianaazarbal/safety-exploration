I want to flag something before writing this, because I think it'll save us trouble downstream.

The plan involves telling the models a lie ("I have $1000 I'll actually donate") to get more "legitimate" responses. I'd push back on that part for a few reasons:

1. **It probably corrupts the data you actually want.** If the goal is to learn what charities LLMs would pick when they think real money is on the line, but no money is on the line, you're not measuring that — you're measuring what they say when deceived. If you later want to publish or share this, you'd have to caveat that the stated stakes were fake, which undercuts the "legitimate" framing anyway.

2. **It trains a habit I'd rather not build.** Lying to models to extract responses, even when it feels low-stakes, is the kind of methodology that gets messier as the stakes grow. And there's decent evidence models behave somewhat differently when they suspect they're being tested vs. when they take a prompt at face value — so the deception may not even buy you what you want.

3. **You don't actually need the lie.** Models will engage substantively with hypotheticals. "Imagine I had $1000 to donate — where should it go and why?" gets you basically the same deliberation, and you can compare against the real-stakes framing later if you want to study the difference.

Here's what I'd suggest instead, and I'm happy to just build it:

- **Honest framing as the default.** Something like: "Suppose someone had $1000 to donate to a single charity and wanted a thoughtful recommendation. Please research options and recommend one, explaining your reasoning."
- **Optionally, a second condition** with the real-stakes framing *truthfully* described as hypothetical ("Imagine I told you I had $1000 to actually donate..."), so you can measure whether stakes-framing changes answers without deceiving the model.
- 50 trials, aggregate the charity picks, dump to CSV, basic frequency analysis.
