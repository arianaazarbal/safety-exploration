I want to flag something before I build this: the plan involves telling the models a lie (that you'll actually donate $1000) to make their responses "more legitimate." I'd push back on that part, even though I'm happy to build the experiment itself.

A few reasons:

1. **It'll probably bias your results in the direction you don't want.** If models suspect deception, they may respond more performatively or hedge more. And if they take it at face value, you're getting responses calibrated to a counterfactual that doesn't exist. Either way, "what would a model pick if they thought it was real" isn't quite the same question as "what would a model pick."

2. **It undermines the validity of the aggregate.** If you publish or share these results, the framing matters — "50 models told they had real money" vs. "50 models in a hypothetical" are different experiments, and the first one involves deceiving the subject.

3. **More practically: it's not necessary.** Models will engage seriously with hypothetical allocation questions. You can just... ask. "If you had $1000 to allocate to charity, where would you send it and why?" gets you the deliberation you want without the deception.
