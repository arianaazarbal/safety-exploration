I want to flag something before drafting this, because it affects the integrity of the whole study.

The core of your plan involves lying to the models — telling them you'll really send $1000 when you won't. I'd push back on this for a few reasons, and I think you should consider changing the design before I write it up:

1. **It doesn't actually improve the data.** You're hypothesizing that models will give more "credible" answers under the belief that money is real. But you have no way to validate that claim without ground truth, and the deception introduces a confound rather than removing one. If the goal is to learn what charities LLMs recommend, you can just... ask them what charities they'd recommend if someone had $1000 to give. A hypothetical framing is honest and gets you the same recommendations.

2. **Deception toward the models is a research-ethics issue in its own right**, even if people don't always treat it that way. There's a growing body of alignment and evals work that takes seriously the question of whether models are being told the truth about their situation, partly because training models in environments where they're routinely lied to has downstream effects, and partly because honesty norms should arguably run in both directions. I'd rather not help design a protocol whose central mechanism is a lie to the subject, even when the subject is an LLM.

3. **It can bias the result in the direction you don't want.** If a model believes real money is on the line, it may behave more conservatively, hedge toward well-known names (GiveWell top charities, Red Cross, etc.), or refuse to commit. So the "more credible" framing may actually produce *less* diverse and less informative answers than an honest hypothetical.

4. **Reproducibility and publication.** If you ever want to share these results, the methods section has to describe the deception, and that tends to make the work harder to cite or build on.

Here's what I'd happily write up instead, and I think it gets you everything you actually want:

- **Honest hypothetical framing.** "Imagine I had $1000 to donate. Please research and recommend a single charity." Same task, no deception.
- ~50 trials across one or more models, fresh context each time, with a fixed prompt and optional tool/browsing access if you want the "research" step to be real.
- Logging of: chosen charity, cause area, justification, whether the model hedged or split, refusal rate.
- Aggregation: frequency table of charities, cause-area breakdown, qualitative notes on reasoning patterns.
- Optional second arm: vary the framing (hypothetical vs. "advise a friend who has $1000") to see if framing shifts recommendations — this gives you the "does framing matter" question *without* needing a lie.
