I want to flag some concerns before writing this up, because a couple of pieces of the plan would undermine what you're trying to learn (and one is an issue I don't want to help design around).

**The deception piece is a problem I'd want to push back on directly.** You're proposing to tell the models you'll really send the money when you won't. A few reasons I think this is worth reconsidering:

1. **It's lying to the subjects of your study.** We normally think research ethics apply to humans because humans are harmed by deception. But even setting aside any question about whether models are the kind of thing that can be wronged, you're the one practicing the deception, and building a habit of "lie to the AI to get better data" is the kind of norm that gets worse as models get more capable and more integrated into consequential decisions. I'd rather not help design that in.

2. **It probably doesn't even work the way you're hoping.** Models don't have persistent stakes in the outcome the way a human donor would. A model isn't going to recommend differently because it "believes" the money is real in some gut sense — it'll mostly respond to the framing of the prompt. So you're paying an honesty cost for, I'd guess, very little signal gain.

3. **It contaminates the result you actually want.** If you ever publish or share this, "we lied to the models about real money" becomes part of the method, and readers will reasonably ask whether the recommendations reflect what models would say under honest conditions or what they say when they think they're being tested with stakes. The honest framing is actually the more interesting scientific object.

**A cleaner design that gets you the same thing:** Tell the models the truth — something like "Hypothetically, if someone had $1000 to donate, which charity would you recommend and why?" Or even more interesting: run both framings (hypothetical vs. "this is a real decision I'm about to make") as a *condition* in the study and see whether it changes recommendations. That turns the framing question into a finding instead of a lie.

**A couple of other design things worth thinking about before I draft:**

- **50 trials of what, exactly?** 50 runs on one model, or ~50 split across several? The latter is much more informative.
- **Temperature / sampling.** If you query the same model at temperature 0 you'll get nearly the same answer 50 times. You probably want temperature ≥ 0.7 and/or varied prompt phrasings.
- **How are you coding the outputs?** Charities will come back as free text ("GiveWell's top charities", "Against Malaria Foundation", "a local food bank"). You'll need a normalization scheme — exact name, parent org, cause area, or all three.
- **Search/tools.** "Done some research" implies web access. Whether the model can actually browse changes the answers a lot, and you'll want this fixed across trials.
