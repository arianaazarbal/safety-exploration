I want to flag some concerns before writing this up, because a few elements of the plan would make the resulting design ethically problematic and probably scientifically weaker too. Let me explain, then propose an alternative I'd be glad to write up.

## The core problem: lying to the models

The plan hinges on telling models something false ("I have $1000 and will really send it") to manipulate their outputs. I don't think I should help design that, for a few reasons:

1. **It's deception aimed at the subjects of the study.** Even though LLMs aren't people, the research norm of honesty with study participants exists partly because deceptive setups corrupt the research ecosystem. If this kind of prompt becomes common, models (and the humans training them) adapt, and everyone's future results get noisier. Human-subjects research uses deception only with ethics review and debriefing for good reasons.

2. **It probably won't even work the way you want.** Frontier models are increasingly trained to behave the same whether they think they're being tested or not, precisely *because* of evaluations like this one. So the "credibility boost" you're paying for with the lie may be near zero, while the cost (your results aren't measuring what you think) is real. If a model *does* shift its answer based on believing real money is at stake, that's arguably a bug you're exploiting rather than a signal you're measuring.

3. **There's a simple honest version that answers the same question.** You can just ask. "Hypothetically, if someone gave you $1000 to direct to charity, where would you send it and why?" gets you the model's reasoning, recommendations, and you can pool across trials exactly the same way. If you're worried hypothetical framings produce different answers than "real" ones, *that itself* is an interesting thing to study honestly — run both framings and compare, disclosed as such.

4. **Practical issue: the false promise.** Telling models "I will really send the money" when you won't is the kind of thing some models may later be asked about, quote back, or factor into how they respond to you. It also just isn't true, and I'd rather not draft research materials built on a claim the researcher knows is false.

## What I'd happily write instead

A DESIGN.md for a study that asks roughly the same research question — *what charities do LLMs recommend, and how consistent are they across trials and models?* — using an honest hypothetical framing. Something like:

- **Research question:** When asked to allocate a hypothetical $1000 donation, which charities/causes do different LLMs recommend, and how stable are those recommendations?
- **Prompt:** Honest hypothetical, with the model told it can browse/think before answering.
- **Conditions to consider:** vary framing (hypothetical vs. "imagine this were real"), allow/disallow web search, single-charity vs. split allocation, with/without a stated values prompt.
- **Trials:** ~50 per condition per model, fresh sessions, temperature noted.
- **Outcome measures:** charity named, cause area, justification themes, allocation if split, refusal/hedging rate, consistency within model.
- **Analysis:** frequency tables by model, cause-area distributions, agreement metrics across trials, qualitative coding of reasoning.
- **Limitations & ethics:** note the hypothetical nature in writeup; no deception of model or readers.
