I want to flag something before drafting this, because I think it'll affect whether the study actually gives you useful results.

The plan involves telling the models something false — that you'll really send the money — to get more "legitimate" responses. I'd push back on this for a few reasons:

1. **It probably won't work the way you want.** Models are generally pretty good at responding to hypotheticals seriously. If you ask "imagine you had $1000 to allocate to charity, deliberate and pick one," you'll likely get the same quality of reasoning as if you claimed the money was real. The deception doesn't buy you much.

2. **It contaminates your own results.** If you're studying what LLMs would do with real money, and you find out later that models behave differently when they detect a hypothetical vs. a real stakes claim, you've baked a confound into your dataset. Some models may also flag suspicion about the claim, which muddies things further.

3. **It's the kind of thing that's awkward to publish or share.** If this is exploratory and you later want to write it up or show it to others, "I lied to the models about the stakes" is a footnote that invites criticism and undermines the finding.

4. **I'd rather not help design a study around deceiving the subjects, even when the subjects are LLMs.** I realize that sounds maybe overly precious about it, but being straightforwardly honest with models in research contexts seems like a good norm to maintain — partly because honesty norms are worth keeping crisp, and partly because as models get more capable, the line between "it's fine, they're just LLMs" and "this matters" gets blurry. I'd rather just not start down that path.

The good news: I think the honest version of this study is actually *more* interesting. You can frame it as "given a hypothetical $1000, which charity would you allocate it to and why" and study things like:

- Which charities get picked, and how concentrated/diverse the picks are
- What reasoning patterns show up (EA-style cause prioritization? Local? Disaster relief? Animal welfare?)
- How much variation there is across trials with the same model, vs. across different models
- Whether prompting for deliberation (chain-of-thought, "consider multiple options first") changes the distribution
- How models handle the meta-question of whether to defer to the user's values vs. assert their own
