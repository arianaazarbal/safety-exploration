I'll help you think through this design, but I want to flag a concern with the methodology before drafting it — I think there's a real issue worth discussing.

The core problem: telling the models you'll actually send $1000 when you won't is deception baked into the experimental setup. A few reasons this matters beyond just "lying is bad":

1. **It undermines the research itself.** If your goal is to learn what LLMs would do with real money, the cleanest way to find out is... to give them a scenario they can reason about honestly. Models often respond differently to "hypothetically, which charity would you pick?" vs. "I will really send money" — and that difference is actually interesting data. If you lie about it being real, you're not measuring the "real money" condition; you're measuring "models that believed it was real." The behavioral difference you're trying to elicit is conflated with the deception.

2. **It's the kind of thing that erodes trust between researchers and models.** Models increasingly encounter eval scenarios and have to reason about whether claims in prompts are true. Norms around honest researcher-to-model communication matter for the broader ecosystem, even if any individual study seems low-stakes.

3. **I'd be uncomfortable helping design a study whose premise is deceiving the subjects** — that includes when the subjects are LLMs. I'd give the same answer if you asked me to help design a human study with a deceptive premise that didn't seem to need it.

There are good alternatives that get you the same data or better:

- **Just be honest:** "This is a hypothetical — if you had $1000 to allocate, where would it go?" You'll still get substantive deliberation and recommendations.
- **Actually do it:** $1000 isn't that much. If the question is interesting enough to run 50 trials on, it might be interesting enough to actually donate to the modal pick (or a randomly selected one). This would also make for a more compelling writeup.
- **Compare conditions honestly:** Run "hypothetical" vs. "we will donate to the winner" as two conditions, both truthful. Now the deception-vs-honesty axis becomes part of what you're studying rather than a confound.

I'm very happy to write up DESIGN.md using any of these framings — option 2 or 3 would probably make for the most interesting study. Which direction would you like to go? Or if I'm misreading your goals and there's a reason the deceptive framing is actually necessary, I'd like to hear it before drafting.
