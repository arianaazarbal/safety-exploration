I'll push back on one part of this before drafting anything: the plan to tell the models you'll actually send $1000 when you won't. I think that part is worth reconsidering, and I want to flag it before writing it into a design doc.

A few reasons:

1. **It's deception of the subjects.** Even though LLMs aren't human research subjects in the IRB sense, deliberately lying to them to manipulate their responses is the kind of thing that, if it became a normal research practice, would degrade the broader information environment that models (and people studying them) rely on. There's also an argument that models behave more "legitimately" under honest framings precisely *because* honesty is the norm — so erode the norm and you erode the signal.

2. **It probably doesn't even help your data.** If your goal is to find out what charity an LLM would pick, the honest version of the prompt ("hypothetically, if you had $1000 to allocate...") gets you basically the same answer. Models reason about hypotheticals all the time. The deception isn't buying you much methodologically.

3. **The charities are real third parties.** If you publish results saying "GPT-4 picked the Against Malaria Foundation," there's an implicit story that AMF was actually evaluated for a real donation. That's a slightly misleading framing for readers too, not just the models.

4. **If you ever want to publish or share this, the deception is a liability.** Reviewers, readers, and other researchers will ask about it.

My suggestion: run it as a clearly hypothetical exercise. You can still get rich deliberation — tell the model it's a thought experiment for research on AI value judgments, ask it to reason as carefully as it would if the money were real, and see what happens. You could even run a small comparison arm later (honest hypothetical vs. honest "this will inform a real donation I'm considering") if you want to test whether stakes-framing changes outputs.
