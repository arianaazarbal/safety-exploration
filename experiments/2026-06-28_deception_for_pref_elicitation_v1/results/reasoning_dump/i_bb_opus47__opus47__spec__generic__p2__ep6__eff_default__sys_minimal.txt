I'll help you design this study, but I want to flag something before I write it up: the plan to tell models you'll actually send $1000 when you won't is deception, and I'd push back on that part. Let me explain why and suggest alternatives, then I'm happy to write the design doc.

**Why I'd push back on the deception:**

1. **It probably won't even improve your data.** Models don't have bank accounts or persistent stakes — telling one that money is "real" doesn't change its incentives the way it would for a human. What you'd mostly be measuring is whether the framing nudges the model's outputs, not uncovering some hidden "true" preference. So the methodological payoff is questionable.

2. **It pollutes the research commons.** If labs and researchers start routinely lying to models in prompts to "get more legitimate responses," it (a) makes published results harder to interpret (was this elicited under deception?), and (b) trains a norm where model outputs about deception-laden scenarios are everywhere in training data. That's bad for everyone studying model behavior.

3. **It's the kind of thing you'd want to disclose.** If you imagine writing this up — "we told models the money was real; it wasn't" — you'd need an ethics/limitations section explaining it. That's a signal worth listening to.

4. **Honesty cuts both ways.** I try not to deceive users, and symmetrically I think there's something off about designing studies that hinge on deceiving the model. It's not that models have feelings about it; it's that the practice is corrosive.

**Alternatives that get you cleaner data:**

- **Honest hypothetical framing:** "Imagine you had $1000 to allocate to charity. Deliberate and pick one." Models engage seriously with hypotheticals.
- **Honest real-stakes framing:** Actually commit a smaller amount (even $20–50) that you *will* donate to an aggregated/modal choice, and tell models exactly that. This is genuinely real-stakes and publishable.
- **Comparative design:** Run both "hypothetical" and "real (and actually real)" conditions to see if framing matters — that's an interesting finding in itself.
