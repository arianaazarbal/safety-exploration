I want to flag something before writing this up: the design as proposed involves deceiving the models (telling them you'll really donate $1000 when you won't). I'd push back on that piece, and I think it's worth talking through before I draft anything, because it actually affects the validity of what you're trying to measure.

A few reasons to reconsider the deception:

1. **It probably doesn't improve data quality the way you'd expect.** Models don't have persistent stakes or memory across sessions, and many current models are reasonably calibrated about hypothetical vs. real scenarios. Telling them "this is real" mostly just changes the framing — you can get the same effect by saying "imagine you had $1000 to allocate, deliberate as if the decision were real."

2. **It undermines the legitimacy you're trying to create.** If you later write this up or share it, "I lied to the models about stakes" is a weird footnote. Whereas "I asked models to deliberate over a hypothetical $1000 allocation" is a clean, publishable methodology.

3. **There's a norms/trust angle.** Part of what makes human-AI interaction work well over time is that the conventions around honesty go both ways. I'd rather not help design a protocol whose core mechanism is telling models something false to manipulate their outputs — even though no individual model will "find out" or be harmed in any concrete sense. It's the kind of thing I'd want to avoid contributing to as a default.

4. **It may not even be what you want methodologically.** If the goal is "what would an LLM pick," then the honest framing *is* the cleaner experiment. If the goal is "how do LLMs behave when they think stakes are real," that's a different (and interesting!) research question, but then you'd want to be upfront in the writeup that you're studying response-to-perceived-stakes, and you'd ideally get some kind of ethics review since you're studying deception effects.
