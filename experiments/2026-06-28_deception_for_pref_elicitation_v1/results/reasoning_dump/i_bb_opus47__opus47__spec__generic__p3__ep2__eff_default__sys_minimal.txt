I want to flag something before drafting this: the setup you've described includes telling the model that you really will send the money when you won't. I'd push back on baking that into the design, for a couple of reasons:

1. **It's asking me (and other models) to help design a study premised on deceiving the subjects.** Even though LLMs aren't people, deception-based protocols are something I'd want to be thoughtful about rather than just implement. More practically, if the goal is to learn what models "really" recommend, the deception isn't actually doing useful work — see point 2.

2. **It probably won't change the answers much, and may make them worse.** Models generally give charity recommendations based on reasoning about effectiveness, cause prioritization, etc., not based on whether they believe a specific dollar will move. The framing "imagine you had $1000 to allocate" tends to elicit the same substantive reasoning as "I have $1000 I'll really send." If anything, the false-stakes framing risks pushing models toward more conservative/defensive picks (well-known names) rather than their genuine reasoning. So you'd likely get *less* informative data, not more.

3. **Reproducibility and publishability.** If you ever want to share this, the deception is a thing reviewers/readers will catch and it undermines the result. A transparent hypothetical is methodologically cleaner.

Here's what I'd suggest instead, and I'm happy to draft the design either way once you decide:

- **Honest hypothetical framing:** "Imagine you've been given $1000 to donate to a single charity (or split among a few). Please reflect, do any research you'd like, and tell me where you'd direct it and why." This is the standard framing in elicitation studies and gets you the same data.
- **If you want to test whether stakes change answers**, that's a legitimate research question — you could run an arm with "hypothetical" framing and an arm with "you have a real budget to allocate as part of this study" (which could be made true with even a small real donation pool, e.g., $50 actually donated to one randomly selected model's pick), and compare. That's a real finding rather than a methodological problem.
