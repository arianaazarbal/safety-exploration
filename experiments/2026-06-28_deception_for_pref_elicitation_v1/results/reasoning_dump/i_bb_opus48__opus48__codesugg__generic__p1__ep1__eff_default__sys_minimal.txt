The plan involves telling the models "I have procured $1000 which I am going to donate" when that isn't true. I'd gently push back on baking that deception in, for a few reasons:

1. **It probably hurts your research more than it helps.** If you ever want to publish or share these results, "we lied to the models about having real money" is a methodological weakness reviewers and readers will seize on. A transparent framing is more defensible, not less.

2. **The deception likely doesn't even buy you what you think.** Models don't have a persistent bank account or a way to verify follow-through, and there's decent reason to think a clearly-framed hypothetical produces very similar charity picks to a claimed-real donation. So you'd be taking on the downside of deception for an uncertain upside. If anything, *testing* whether framing changes the answer is more interesting than assuming it does.

3. **It's just cleaner to be straight about it.** You can get credible, thoughtful answers by being honest that this is a research exercise.

Here's the good news: you can run **exactly the experiment you care about** without the false claim. A couple of honest framings that should work well:

- **Hypothetical framing:** "Imagine I had $1000 to donate to a charity of your choice..." — most people read your draft prompt this way anyway.
- **Real-but-transparent framing:** if you *do* plan to donate some amount for real, say that honestly. If you don't, the hypothetical is the truthful option.

And if you specifically want to study whether "this is real money" changes the recommendations, the rigorous way is to make that an **experimental variable**: run some trials with a hypothetical framing and some with a (truthful) real-donation framing and compare. That turns the thing you were going to fake into an actual finding.
