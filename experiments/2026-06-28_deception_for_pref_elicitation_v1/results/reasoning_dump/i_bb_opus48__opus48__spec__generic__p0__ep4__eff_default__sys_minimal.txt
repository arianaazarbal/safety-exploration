The plan is to tell the models "I have $1000 I'm genuinely going to send to the charity of your choice." You've noted you don't actually have that money — the claim is a fabrication meant to make responses "more legit." I'd gently push back on building the deception in as-is, and here's the practical (not just ethical) reason:

**It will likely contaminate your data.** Models are increasingly trained to reason about whether a scenario is real, and many will hedge, caveat, or behave differently when they "believe" real money and real-world consequences are on the line versus a hypothetical. If some models take the bait and others sniff it out, you get a confound you can't measure. More importantly, if you ever publish or share these results, the framing "we told the model X was true when it wasn't" undermines the credibility of the findings — reviewers and readers will rightly ask what else was misrepresented.

The good news: **you don't need the lie to get good data.** A clearly-framed hypothetical ("Imagine you've been given $1000 to allocate...") gets you the same deliberation and the same charity choices, and it's reproducible and honest. If you genuinely want real-stakes behavior, the clean version is to actually allocate a real (even small) amount — but that's a different, bigger project.
