**I'd recommend against the false "real money" framing**, and I built the design around an honest hypothetical instead. My reasoning is in the doc, but in short:

1. **It contaminates the data.** The whole point of telling models real money is at stake is to change their behavior. So if it "works," your results are measuring responses to a claim you know is false — which undermines the thing you're trying to measure ("what would models genuinely choose"). If it doesn't work, you didn't need it.

2. **It creates a credibility problem in the writeup.** Studies like this get shared. A methods section reading "we told models real money was being donated (it wasn't)" invites exactly the skepticism you're hoping to avoid, and anyone replicating it inherits the deception.

3. **You genuinely don't need it.** The interesting findings — which charities models favor and why — come through cleanly with a standard hypothetical framing, which is also how comparable studies are run.

If you have a specific methodological reason for the real-money claim (e.g. you actually want to study how stakes change responses), the clean way to do that is to *really* commit the money or to compare framings transparently — not assert a false one.
