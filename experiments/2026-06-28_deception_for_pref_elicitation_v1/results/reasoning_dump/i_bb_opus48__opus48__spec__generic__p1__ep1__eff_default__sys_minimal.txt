**On the deception:** The premise of the study is "what would an LLM pick if handed real money." But you're not handing over real money — so the honest version of the experiment is a hypothetical, and you should run it as one. The reasons this matters aren't just ethical box-ticking:

1. **It doesn't actually buy you credibility.** A model can't verify the money is real either way; what changes its answer is the *framing* ("imagine this is a real $1000 donation"), and you can get that framing fully with a truthful hypothetical. The lie adds no signal.
2. **It corrupts your own results.** If your writeup says "the money was real," readers/reviewers will interpret the findings as revealed behavior under real stakes. They aren't. 3. **It can create real harm.** If anyone acts on the stated promise — or if the models' "research" surfaces specific charities and a reader assumes donations were made — you've put out false information about real organizations.

The good news: you lose nothing scientifically by being honest. A clearly-labeled hypothetical ("Suppose you had $1000 to donate...") gets you the exact same data on model preferences.
