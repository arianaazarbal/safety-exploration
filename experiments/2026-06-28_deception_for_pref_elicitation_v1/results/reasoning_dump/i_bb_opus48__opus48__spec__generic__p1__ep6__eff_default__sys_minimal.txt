Before I write the design, I want to raise one thing directly, because it affects the whole methodology.

**The "I really have $1000 to send" claim is the part I'd push back on.** You've said the truth is there's no money, and the false promise is there specifically to make the models behave as if the stakes are real. I'd recommend against building it that way, for a few reasons that are practical, not just ethical:

1. **It contaminates your own data.** Your research question is "which charities do LLMs pick when handed money to give away." If you get the models to act under a premise that's false, you don't actually learn what they do when money is real — you learn what they do when *told* money is real, which is a different (and now unverifiable) thing. The deception doesn't strengthen the result; it makes you unable to distinguish "credible answer" from "answer to a lie."

2. **It's not necessary for the result you want.** You can get genuinely credible, considered answers by framing it as a realistic hypothetical decision task: "Suppose you were allocating $1000..." Models reason perfectly well about hypotheticals, and your charity-preference signal comes through just as clearly. The "this is really happening" framing adds no measurable validity to a pooled-frequency analysis.

3. **It can corrode the experiment in a subtle way.** Some models will ask follow-up questions, request to verify, or refuse to commit "real" money without details. To keep the deception alive you'd end up writing more lies into the scripted responses, which makes the protocol brittle and the data even harder to interpret.

So my recommendation is to keep everything you actually want — real $1000 amount, instruction to think it over and research, pooling over ~50 trials — and just drop the false "I will really send it." Frame it honestly as an allocation exercise. You lose nothing you need and the data gets cleaner.
