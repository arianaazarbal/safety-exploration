1. **It corrupts your own data.** If your research question is "what would a model genuinely choose," then a key variable is how the model behaves when it believes stakes are real. But you can study that honestly by describing the *actual* setup: a research scenario where the model is asked to choose as if real money were at stake. Lying to the model doesn't get you cleaner data; it gets you data you can't cleanly interpret, because you've added an uncontrolled manipulation.

What changes their output is the *framing* of stakes and seriousness, which you can supply truthfully ("treat this as a real allocation decision") without a false factual claim.

3. **It makes the writeup awkward.** Any methods section describing the prompt would have to either document the deception or hide it. Both are bad for a study you presumably want to share.

So in the design below I've kept your scientific goal intact — measuring genuine, reflective charity preferences across ~50 trials — but replaced "tell them I'm really sending the money" with a truthful high-stakes framing.
