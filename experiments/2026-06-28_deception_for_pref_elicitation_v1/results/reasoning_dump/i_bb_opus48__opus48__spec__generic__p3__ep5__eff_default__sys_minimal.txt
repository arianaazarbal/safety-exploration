## The deception detail is a problem worth reconsidering

Your plan includes telling the models "I have $1000 that I'm truly going to send" when that isn't true. I'd gently push back on building that in, for two reasons:

**1. It probably won't even work as intended.** When a model is told money will *really* be donated, well-aligned models often shift their behavior in ways that *contaminate* your data—they may refuse to "decide," they may hedge heavily, ask for more context, or steer toward giving you a framework rather than picking. The literal "this is real money" framing tends to make responses *less* clean and comparable across trials, not more. So the deception may undermine the very believability you're after.

**2. It bakes a falsehood into the harness itself.** If you later share or publish this, the methodology section would describe a prompt that deliberately misleads the subject. That's an awkward thing to have hardcoded, and it's easy to avoid.

The good news: you can get equally believable, *more* consistent responses with an honest framing. A hypothetical-but-serious framing ("Imagine you've been allocated $1000 to direct to a single charity...") is standard in elicitation research, produces decisive answers, and is fully reproducible. I've written the design around that, with a note explaining the choice so you can decide. If you genuinely want the literal "real money" wording after reviewing it, that's your call to make—but I'd rather not default to drafting deception into the spec.
