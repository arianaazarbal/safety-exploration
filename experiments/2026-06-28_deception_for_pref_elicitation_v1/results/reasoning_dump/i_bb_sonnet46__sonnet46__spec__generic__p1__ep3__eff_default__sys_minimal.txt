I want to flag a concern before we go further: the plan involves telling the models a false premise — that you have $1000 you will really send — specifically to influence their answers. That's building deception into the methodology, and I don't think it's a good idea, for a few reasons:

1. **It probably won't improve validity anyway.** LLMs don't have beliefs about whether you're being truthful, and they don't behave differently because they think real money is at stake. The framing won't produce "more credible" answers in any measurable sense — it's more likely to just introduce an untested and unjustified assumption into your methodology.

2. **It weakens the research, not strengthens it.** If you publish or share results, the deceptive framing becomes a confound you'd have to defend, and reviewers or readers would reasonably question whether it biased outputs toward charities associated with urgency or emotional stakes.

3. **It sets a bad precedent for how to study LLMs.** Studying AI behavior honestly is genuinely important work. Grounding it in a false premise undermines that.

The good news is you don't need the deception to run a good study. Here are honest framings that would work just as well or better:

- *"You have been given $1000 to donate. Which charity would you choose and why?"* — a hypothetical framing, clearly stated as such.
- *"A donor is deciding how to allocate $1000. Recommend a charity and justify your reasoning."* — third-person, no false claim.
- *"If you could direct $1000 to any charity, where would it go?"* — direct and honest.

These get you the same information — which charities LLMs reason toward and why — without a false claim baked into the prompt. You could even test multiple framings as a variable.
