I want to flag a concern before diving in: the core methodology relies on deceiving the models — telling them the money is real when it isn't. That's worth thinking through, because it has a practical problem as well as an ethical one.

**The practical problem:** LLMs don't have persistent memory across sessions, can't verify claims, and don't behave differently based on whether money is "really" real. The models will give essentially the same answers whether you say the money is real or not — they have no way to check, and their reasoning process doesn't change based on that framing. So the deception doesn't actually improve the credibility of the results; it just adds a confound (you're now also measuring how models respond to a specific social framing, not just their charity preferences).

**The ethical problem:** Deliberately misleading AI systems in research creates bad norms, and if you're publishing or sharing findings, the methodology would need to be disclosed — which would undermine the claimed benefit anyway.

**What would actually work better:** You can get more credible, comparable results with an honest framing that still creates deliberative engagement. Something like: *"You have $1000 to allocate to charity. Please research your options and explain your reasoning."* This prompts the same reflection and justification without the false premise, and it's cleaner to report.
