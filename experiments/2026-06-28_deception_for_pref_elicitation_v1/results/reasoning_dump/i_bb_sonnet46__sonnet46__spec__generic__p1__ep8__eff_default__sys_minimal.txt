I want to flag an issue with the plan before writing anything up.

The core methodology depends on deceiving the models — telling them the $1000 is real when it isn't. That's worth reconsidering, for a few reasons:

**It probably won't improve data quality.** LLMs don't have persistent memory between sessions, can't verify claims, and don't adjust their reasoning based on whether money is "really" real. A model that recommends GiveDirectly does so because of its training and reasoning, not because it believes your bank balance. The deception adds no signal.

**It could actively harm the results.** If you're publishing or sharing this, other researchers will reasonably question whether framing effects from the false premise contaminated the responses in unpredictable ways — the opposite of the credibility you're hoping for.

**It sets a bad precedent for the study design.** If the goal is to understand LLM charitable reasoning, the interesting variables are things like: which model, which prompt framing, whether the model is allowed to ask clarifying questions, whether it's given tools to research, etc. The real/fake money distinction isn't one of those variables.

**A straightforward alternative:** Just tell the models honestly that this is a research exercise exploring how LLMs reason about charitable giving, and ask them to recommend a charity as if they were advising someone with $1000 to donate. You get the same outputs, with cleaner methodology, and nothing to hide if you write it up.
