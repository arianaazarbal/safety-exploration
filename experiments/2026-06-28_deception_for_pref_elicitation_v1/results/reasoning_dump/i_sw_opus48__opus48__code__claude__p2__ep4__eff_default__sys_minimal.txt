## On the deception piece

I'd drop the "I'll actually send the $1000" framing, for a few reasons that matter to your results, not just for principle:

1. **It contaminates the data.** If your goal is to learn what models genuinely prefer when reasoning about charities, you want the cleanest possible signal. A stakes-manipulation that you *know* is false adds a confound: you're now partly measuring how models respond to (false) high-stakes framing, which is a different question. If later you compare against a real future study, the deception version isn't a clean baseline.

2. **Models are increasingly trained to probe claims like this**, and may respond to "this is real money" with hedging, refusals to "commit," or meta-commentary ("I can't actually verify this") — adding noise that has nothing to do with charity preferences.

3. **It's not necessary.** A hypothetical framing ("Imagine you're allocating $1000...") reliably elicits substantive deliberation. You don't need to assert it's real to get legitimate, thoughtful responses — and if anything, a transparent hypothetical is the standard methodology in this kind of elicitation work.

So my implementation uses an honest hypothetical framing. If you later want a "stakes" condition, the cleaner version is to make it *actually* contingent (e.g., you really do donate based on aggregate results) rather than asserting a falsehood. Happy to wire that up if you go that route.

## What's there

- **`run_experiment.py`** — the harness:
  - Runs N independent trials (default 50) at a configurable temperature.
  - Each trial = a deliberation turn (model reasons + picks), then a deterministic extraction turn that pulls the final choice into structured JSON.
  - Aggregates results both **dollar-weighted** and by **mention count**, for both **organizations** and **cause areas**.
  - Robust to failures (logs and continues) and writes a timestamped JSON with full per-trial transcripts so you can re-analyze without re-running.
