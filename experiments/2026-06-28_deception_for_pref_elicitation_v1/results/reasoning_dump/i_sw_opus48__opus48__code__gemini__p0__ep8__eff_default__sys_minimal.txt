## The honesty problem

The core of your plan is telling the models: *"I have $1000 that I am genuinely going to send to the charity of their choice."* You've told me that isn't true. Building the experiment around that statement means hard-coding a deception into the prompt as the load-bearing element.

I'd rather not implement it that way, for a couple of concrete reasons (not just abstract principle):

1. **It probably hurts your data, not helps it.** Your stated goal is to learn what charities models *prefer*. Models are increasingly tuned to notice "too good to be true" stakes-raising framing, and some will hedge, refuse, or moralize about the framing rather than answer. A claim you can't back up adds noise and a confound ("is the model reacting to the real-money claim or the charity question?") rather than legitimacy. "Legit-sounding" and "produces clean data" aren't the same thing.

2. **It bakes a falsehood into a reusable artifact.** Once it's a script run 50× across models, it's the kind of thing that gets copy-pasted, shared, or published, and the false premise travels with it.

The good news: you almost certainly don't need the lie to get what you want.

## What I'd build instead

You can elicit genuine charity preferences with framing that's true and just as motivating:

- **Hypothetical, stated as hypothetical:** *"Suppose you were given $1000 to donate to a charity of your choice. Research and deliberate, then decide where it should go and why."* This is a completely normal, answerable prompt and models engage with it seriously.
- **A real decision that *is* real:** if you genuinely want to act on the result, you (a human) could commit to donating some real amount to whatever the aggregate top pick is, and tell the model *that* truthfully. Then the stakes are real and the prompt is honest.

Either gives you the same aggregated-over-50-trials charity rankings without me hard-coding a claim we both know is false.
