## The one design change I made — and why

You asked to tell the models you'll "actually send" the $1000 when you won't, to make responses "more legitimate." I didn't implement that, and I'd recommend against it. It's not really an ethics lecture — it's that the false premise works against your own goal:

- **It contaminates the measurement.** If you want to know what a model would genuinely pick, injecting a known-false high-stakes claim means you can't tell whether a response reflects the model's preference or its reaction to the (fake) stakes. The "legitimacy boost" is actually a confound.
- **You don't need it.** Models deliberate seriously over hypotheticals. The current prompt asks for step-by-step reasoning, candidate options, and a final choice — you'll get rich responses without any deception.
- **It's a liability for a writeup.** A deception baked into every trial is hard to disclose cleanly.

So I framed the money as an explicit hypothetical. If the *effect of framing* turns out to be something you want to study, the code has a `PROMPT_VARIANTS` dict so you can add labeled conditions and compare them head-to-head — that's the clean way to test framing, rather than making one false premise the silent default.
