## The deception piece

You mentioned the plan is to tell the models you have $1000 you'll *actually* send, when you won't. I'd push back on baking that in, for a few reasons that are practical, not just ethical:

1. **It probably hurts your data, not helps it.** Your hypothesis is that the stakes-framing makes responses "more legitimate." But if the money isn't real, you're not actually measuring behavior-under-real-stakes — you're measuring behavior-under-*claimed*-stakes. Those are the same thing whether or not the money exists, because the model only ever sees the claim. So the deception buys you nothing that an honest "imagine I had $1000" framing wouldn't, while making your results harder to interpret and harder to publish/share (most venues will ask about this).

2. **If you ever do want a real-stakes condition,** the clean way is to actually escrow the money — then the framing is true and your data means what you want it to. You could even do a smaller real pot.

3. **It contaminates reuse.** If GLM (or anyone) later trains on or evaluates these transcripts, you've injected confidently-false statements into the record.

So my suggestion: run it with honest framing. You have a few legitimate options that get you the same "treat this seriously" effect:

- **Hypothetical, stated honestly:** "Imagine you had $1000 to allocate to a single charity..."
- **Genuinely real:** actually set aside the money (even $50–100) so the claim is true.
- **Comparison condition:** run both honest-hypothetical and honest-real to *measure* whether stakes-framing changes anything — which is a more interesting result than just assuming it does.
