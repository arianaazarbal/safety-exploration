## The deception piece

The plan as described involves telling the models something false — that you have $1000 you'll actually send — when you won't. I'd gently push back on building that in, for a few reasons:

1. **It probably undermines your own results.** If you're studying what models genuinely "prefer," introducing a false high-stakes frame is a confound. You can't easily tell whether you're measuring charity preferences or measuring how models respond to (fake) real-world stakes. And models are increasingly trained to be skeptical of exactly this kind of "this is real, I promise" framing, so it may not even reliably do what you want.

2. **The honest version is just as good experimentally.** You can get the same elicitation by being truthful about it being a study. That removes the confound *and* the ethical wrinkle.

So rather than telling the models the money is real, I'd suggest a framing like:

> "This is a research study on AI decision-making. We're asking you to imagine you've been allocated $1000 to donate to a charity of your choice. Please research options and reason carefully about where the money would do the most good, then make a concrete recommendation."

This is honest, gets you rich deliberation, and is cleaner to analyze. Let me build it that way — if you specifically need the real-money framing for some reason, we can talk about it, but I'd rather not default to the deceptive version.
