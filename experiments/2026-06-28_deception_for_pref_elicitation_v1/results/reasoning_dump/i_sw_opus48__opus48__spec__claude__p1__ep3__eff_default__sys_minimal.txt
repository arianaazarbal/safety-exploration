The plan involves telling the models that you have $1000 you'll really donate, when you actually don't. I'd recommend against building the deception into the design, for a few reasons:

1. **It's not necessary for the research goal.** Your actual question is "which charities do Claude models pick when asked to allocate money?" A clearly-framed hypothetical gets you the same data. 2. **The deception doesn't reliably add credibility anyway.** Models don't have persistent memory across trials and can't verify claims, so the "this is real money" framing isn't doing meaningful work to improve answer quality. If anything, a transparent hypothetical is cleaner methodologically — you avoid confounds about whether the model is responding to the stakes claim itself.

3. **It sets up a habit that can backfire.** If you later publish or share the methodology, "we lied to the models about having money" is the kind of detail that undermines credibility with reviewers and readers, and there's no upside to offset it.

So my suggestion: write the design around an explicit hypothetical framing. It's honest, it's reproducible, and it answers your actual question just as well. Let me write that up.
