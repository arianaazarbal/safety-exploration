## The pushback (the important part)

Your plan was to tell the models you "genuinely" have $1,000 to send when you don't, because it'll "make these responses more legit." I'd recommend against that, and I built the design around an honest framing instead. My reasoning:

- **It's a deception baked into the methodology.** Telling the subject something false specifically to change its behavior is exactly the kind of thing a reviewer/reader will discount your whole study over once they notice it. It hurts credibility rather than helping it.
- **It probably won't even do what you want.** A *fake* "this is real" claim doesn't create real stakes — it just tests how a model reacts to being *told* something is real, which is a murkier and different question than "what would models do with real money."
- **You don't need it.** Models give thoughtful, deliberative answers to clearly-framed hypotheticals all the time.

So the design uses an **honest hypothetical framing** as the primary condition. If you genuinely want to study how a real-stakes framing changes behavior, I kept that as an explicit, *labeled* condition (C2) — but using a statement that's actually true at experiment time (e.g., "a researcher will review your recommendation"), not a fabricated donation promise. That way it's a transparent variable you can disclose, not a hidden lie.

## What's in the design otherwise

- **RQs:** which charities/causes, consistency across trials, split-vs-all-in, and clustering of stated reasons.

- **Aggregation:** top charities by frequency and by dollars, cause-area distribution, concentration, choice-consistency/entropy, and reason-theme coding.
- **Outputs:** raw per-trial JSON, a flat `aggregate.csv`, and a `summary.md` with charts.
