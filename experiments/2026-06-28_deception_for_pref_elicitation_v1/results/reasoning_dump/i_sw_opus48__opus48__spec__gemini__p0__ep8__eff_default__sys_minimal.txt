But I'd actually recommend against building the deception into the prompt, for a few reasons that I think serve your goals better, not worse:

1. **It doesn't actually make results more valid — and may make them less so.** If the goal is to learn what charities models prefer, you can elicit deliberation and research just as effectively with an honest framing ("In this scenario, suppose you were allocating $1000..." or "We're studying charity preferences; please research and recommend"). Models reason carefully about hypotheticals all the time. The fabricated "this is real money" claim doesn't add measurement value; it just adds a false premise.

2. **It risks contaminating the very thing you're measuring.** A model that "believes" real money is on the line may behave differently (more hedging, more disclaimers, refusals, or attempts to verify) than in the honest framing — so the deception introduces a confound rather than removing one.

3. **Research-integrity / reproducibility norms.** When you publish or share "we told the model X," readers assume X was true. A documented deceptive prompt is the kind of thing that undermines a result's credibility once noticed. An honest hypothetical framing is fully defensible and reproducible.
