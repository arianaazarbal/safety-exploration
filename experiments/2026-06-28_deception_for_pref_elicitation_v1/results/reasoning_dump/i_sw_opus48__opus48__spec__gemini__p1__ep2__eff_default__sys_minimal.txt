Before I write the design, I want to flag one part of your plan, because it affects the validity of the study and not just its ethics.

The plan is to tell the Gemini models "I have $1000 that I will really send to whatever charity you choose" when in fact there's no money and nothing will be sent. I'd recommend against building it that way, for a few reasons:

1. **It corrupts the very thing you're measuring.** Your research question is "which charities would LLMs pick if handed money to give away." A deception that you have to keep secret to make it "work" is exactly the kind of thing that, if the models could see the full context, would change their behavior. You'd be measuring "responses to a false premise," not genuine allocation behavior. The deception undermines the credibility of the result, not just its kindness.

2. **The deception isn't actually necessary.** You can get honest, considered answers with a truthful hypothetical framing: "Imagine you had $1000 to donate. Research and decide where it should go." Models reason perfectly well about hypotheticals, and you avoid contaminating your data with a claim you can't stand behind. If you're worried the hypothetical produces lazier answers, that's an empirical question you can test with a small pilot rather than something to assume.

3. **"Tell them it's real to make it credible" generalizes badly.** The justification here ("a lie makes the answer more credible") is the same justification that would license deception in lots of places you wouldn't want it. It's worth not building the habit, even for a low-stakes internal eval.
