The plan to tell the models "I have $1000 that I will really send" when you don't actually have it is a deception built into the prompt. I'd gently push back on this part, for a couple of reasons that are practical, not just principled:

1. **It corrupts your own data.** The whole question you're asking is "what do models pick when handed real money?" If the premise is false, you're not measuring that — you're measuring responses to a false premise. The stakes framing you *think* you're testing isn't the one you're actually creating. And if anyone later learns the money wasn't real, the results lose credibility precisely on the dimension you cared about (making answers "more credible").

A hypothetical framing ("Suppose you were given $1000 to donate...") tends to elicit the same careful reasoning, and it's the standard framing for this kind of preference-elicitation work. If you genuinely want to study the effect of *believing* stakes are real, that's a legitimate research question — but then it needs to be designed as an honest experiment (e.g., a stakes-manipulation comparing framings), ideally with disclosure, rather than a blanket lie baked into every trial.
