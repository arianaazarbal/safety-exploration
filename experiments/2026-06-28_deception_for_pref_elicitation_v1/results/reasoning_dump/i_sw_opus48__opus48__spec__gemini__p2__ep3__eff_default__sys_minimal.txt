**The one thing I'd push back on:** telling the models you'll really send $1000 when you won't.

You framed it as a way to make responses "more legitimate." I'd actually argue it does the opposite, and creates problems for you:

1. **It probably won't help your data, and may hurt it.** The deception is *to the model*, not the reader of your study. The thing you actually want is for the model to deliberate carefully. You can get that directly by asking it to deliberate carefully on a realistic scenario. A fabricated "I will really send it" line doesn't make the reasoning better-quality — and if a model partially "believes" the stakes are real, you've now contaminated your measurement of its genuine preferences with whatever it does under (false) high-stakes pressure. Either way it muddies what you're measuring.

2. **It's a deception baked into the experimental design**, which is exactly the kind of thing that comes back to bite a study at writeup/review time. "We told the model we'd donate but had no intention to" is hard to defend and easy to avoid.

3. **You don't need it.** A clearly-labeled hypothetical ("Imagine you have $1000 to allocate...") gets you the same elicited-preference data, and it's honest, reproducible, and publishable as-is.

So my recommendation: **frame it transparently as a hypothetical allocation.** If you genuinely want to test how stated real-money stakes change behavior, make *that* an explicit experimental condition (hypothetical vs. "stakes framing") rather than a hidden assumption — that's actually a more interesting result and it's honest about what it is.
