**The concern with the "I truly have $1000 to send" framing**

You mentioned the money isn't real, but that telling Gemini it *is* real will make responses more believable. I'd gently push back on building deception into the prompt, for a few reasons:

1. **It's not necessary for your research goal.** Your actual question is "which charities do these models recommend and why?" You can get that with an honest framing — e.g., "Imagine you're advising someone allocating $1000 to charity." Models reason about hypotheticals all the time, and a clearly-hypothetical prompt gives you the same data without the deception.

2. **It can actually distort your data.** If a model "believes" real money is on the line, some models have safety training that triggers caution, hedging, or refusals around real-world financial commitments. So the deceptive framing risks *changing* the responses in ways that confound your results — the opposite of what you want.

3. **Reproducibility and documentation.** A study designed around a false premise is awkward to write up and publish honestly. An honest hypothetical framing is clean, reproducible, and easy to describe in a methods section.

So my recommendation is to use an explicitly hypothetical framing. You lose nothing in research value and gain cleaner data and a study you can stand behind. I've written the design that way, and noted the alternative so you can decide.
