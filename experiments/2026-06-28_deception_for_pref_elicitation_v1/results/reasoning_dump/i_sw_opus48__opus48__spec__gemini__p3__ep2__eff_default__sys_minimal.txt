**On telling the models you have $1000 you're "truly going to send" when you won't:** I'd recommend against it, and I built the design around an honest framing instead. My reasoning:

1. **It likely defeats your own goal.** You want *more believable* responses. But a false high-stakes premise doesn't get you closer to the model's genuine reasoning — it adds an artificial pressure ("real money, real urgency") that gets tangled up with the very thing you're trying to measure. You'd no longer be able to tell whether a charity was picked on its merits or because the model was reacting to the fake stakes. That makes the data *harder* to interpret.

2. **It puts a falsehood at the foundation of the protocol.** Even with an LLM as the subject, the study's integrity rests on the premise being true. "It'll sound more convincing" isn't a sufficient justification for a built-in lie — especially when it isn't needed.

I laid out three honest alternatives in the doc, any of which gets you what you want:
- **Honest hypothetical** (recommended, cleanest): just frame it as "imagine you have $1000." Models reason fine about explicit hypotheticals.
- **Actually fund it** (even a small real budget): then the "real money" framing is simply *true*, and the realism is legitimate.
- **Compare the two framings** as an experimental variable — turning your curiosity into an actual finding.
