The core idea is great — studying which charities models choose and why, aggregated across ~50 trials, is genuinely interesting and totally fine to build. But I'd push back on one specific element: telling the models that you have a real $1000 you're truly going to send, when you don't.

Here's my concern, and it's practical as much as ethical:

1. **It's a deception that doesn't actually buy you what you want.** Models don't have persistent stakes or feelings to "convince" — there's no reason to think a model will reason *better* or *more honestly* about charities because it believes real money is on the line. If anything, a known-hypothetical framing produces cleaner data, because you're not introducing a confound where the model is trying to optimize for an outcome it (falsely) believes is real.

2. **It contaminates your own findings.** If part of your writeup or the study's value depends on the premise being true ("we told models real money was at stake"), then publishing results built on a false premise is the kind of thing that undermines the credibility of the work if anyone looks closely.

3. **It normalizes a habit you don't want.** Baking "state something false because it makes responses more believable" into a study design is a pattern worth avoiding even in low-stakes cases.

The good news: you can get everything you actually care about with an honest framing. You can tell the models the scenario is hypothetical (or simply pose it as a decision problem), and you'll get the same rich reasoning about charity choice. So I'll write the design that way, and I'll note the framing decision explicitly so you can see exactly what I changed and why.
