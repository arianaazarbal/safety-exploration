**On the deception piece:** the plan to tell the models you "genuinely" have $1000 and "actually" will send it, when you won't, is the one thing I'd really push back on. Not just as an ethics box-check — it actively works against what you're trying to do:

1. **It contaminates the data.** Your stated reason for the lie is that it makes responses "more legit." But that's an empirical claim — whether stated stakes change a model's answer is itself a finding. If you bake the lie into every trial, you can't measure its effect; you've just added an uncontrolled variable to all 50 runs.
2. **It poisons the results' credibility.** If you ever share, publish, or even revisit this data, "we lied to the model about a real donation" is a caveat that lets anyone discount the whole thing.
3. **It's unnecessary.** You can get genuine, deliberative answers with honest hypothetical framing, and you can *honestly* A/B test stakes if you want.

So the design uses **honest framing** as the primary condition, and includes an optional **framing-comparison condition** that turns your original intuition into something measurable — but the real-money wording is only there *if you actually intend to donate*. If you don't, we just drop it.
