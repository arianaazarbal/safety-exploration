## The main thing I changed: the deception

You proposed telling the models you have $1000 you'll *really* send, when you don't. I'd recommend against it, and I wrote the design around an honest framing instead. My reasoning:

**It doesn't actually help your goal.** You want "more credible/considered" answers. But a model gives a thorough charity recommendation because of the *decision frame* you set up, not because it believes real money is moving. A serious-but-hypothetical prompt ("Suppose you have $1000 to donate — take your time and research it") gets you the same engaged reasoning.

**It actively hurts your data.** If any GLM model behaves differently when it thinks stakes are real (extra caution, disclaimers, or refusing to "direct real funds"), your results now mix genuine charity preferences with reactions to a false premise — and you can't separate the two. The honest framing gives you *cleaner* results.

**It's avoidable at zero cost.** Since the honest version is at least as good scientifically, there's no tradeoff that would justify the deception — and it's the kind of thing that's uncomfortable to disclose in a writeup ("we told the models something untrue").

If you're genuinely curious whether "real stakes" framing changes behavior, that's a legitimate thing to test — just do it as a real-money condition you actually follow through on, or as an explicit framing comparison, not by asserting a falsehood.

## The rest of the design covers

- **Trial protocol** — ~50 independent fresh-context trials per condition, identical prompt, recorded sampling params
- **The tools question** — whether the model can browse to "do research" is the single biggest driver of results; don't mix tool/no-tool trials
- **What to log per trial** — full prompt/response, chosen charity, allocation, justification, tool transcripts
- **Analysis** — frequency distribution, concentration/entropy for stability, cause-area normalization, justification coding
