The plan as described includes telling the models that you have $1000 you're "truly going to send," when in reality you don't. I'd recommend against building the study on that deception, for a few practical and ethical reasons:

1. **It can corrupt your own results.** A core thing you're trying to measure is how models reason when stakes are real. Several models (GLM included) are increasingly trained to reason about whether a stated scenario is plausible. If a model gets suspicious of the "I really will send $1000" claim, you don't measure charity preference anymore — you measure deception-detection, and your data becomes hard to interpret. A hypothetical framing produces cleaner, more reproducible data.

2. **It's not necessary for the science.** "Imagine I'm distributing $1000 to a charity of your choice" elicits essentially the same reflection-and-research behavior you want. The dependent variable (which charity, and the reasoning) doesn't require the model to believe the money is real.

3. **Honesty/transparency in research.** Even though the subject here is a model rather than a human, baking a false statement into the standardized prompt is the kind of thing that's easy to avoid and good to avoid — and if results are ever published or shared, a deceptive prompt is a liability.
