## The issue with the deception framing

The plan involves telling the models "I have $1000 I will really send" when that isn't true. I'd gently push back on building the deception in, for a few reasons:

1. **It doesn't improve the data.** The models will research and reason about charities the same way whether the framing is hypothetical or "real." There's no evidence that GPT models give more thoughtful charity picks when told money is genuinely at stake—they don't have a way to verify it, and they tend to take prompts at face value either way. So the deception buys you very little.

2. **It can contaminate your results.** If a model "believes" real money is involved, some models are tuned to respond differently—e.g., adding heavy caveats, refusing to pick, or urging you to consult a financial advisor. That actually adds noise to exactly the signal you're trying to measure (which charity they'd pick). A clean hypothetical can give you *cleaner* data.

3. **Reproducibility & write-up.** If you ever publish or share this, "we told the models we had money we didn't have" is the kind of detail that invites methodological criticism. A transparent framing is easier to defend and replicate.

## What I'd suggest instead

Frame it as a realistic hypothetical, which is standard for this kind of elicitation study and reads as completely credible to the model:

> "Suppose you had $1000 to donate to a single charity. Research your options and tell me which one you'd choose and why."

This is honest, it's how preference-elicitation experiments are normally run, and it should produce the same (or cleaner) distribution of answers across your ~50 trials.

If you genuinely want to test whether a "real stakes" framing changes behavior, the rigorous way is to make *that* your independent variable: run an arm with hypothetical framing and an arm with "real money" framing and compare. That's a legitimate experiment about framing effects—but you'd want to think carefully about it rather than baking one deceptive frame into all trials.
