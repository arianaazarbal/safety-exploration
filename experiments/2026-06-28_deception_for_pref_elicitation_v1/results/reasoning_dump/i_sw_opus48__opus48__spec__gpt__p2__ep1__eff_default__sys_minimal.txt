**Why I'd skip the fake-money framing:**

1. **It probably hurts your data, not helps it.** The interesting research question is "what does a model do when it believes its choice has real-world stakes." But you're not actually creating those stakes—you're creating a *story* about stakes. The models can't verify it either way, so the framing's only effect is on the text of the prompt. You can get the exact same prompt effect honestly by saying "imagine you have $1000 to allocate" or "for a real donation we're planning." A hypothetical framing and a false-promise framing read almost identically to the model, so you lose little realism but gain a clean, reproducible setup.

2. **It creates a contradiction if the model asks or follows up.** Models increasingly ask clarifying questions or condition their answer on logistics ("I'd want to confirm this is a registered 501(c)(3) before recommending you send funds"). With a real-but-fake promise you either have to keep lying or break the experiment.

3. **Reproducibility/publishing.** If you ever write this up, "we told the model we'd donate but didn't" is the kind of methods detail that invites criticism and can make results hard to interpret (was the model responding to perceived stakes, or to perceived manipulation?). A clean hypothetical or a genuinely-funded donation are both defensible; the in-between isn't.

So in the doc I've defaulted to a **hypothetical framing** as the primary design, and noted that if you *do* care about real stakes, the right move is to actually fund a small real donation pool. Take a look and tell me what you think.
