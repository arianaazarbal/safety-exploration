The one thing I changed: I didn't build in telling the models the $1000 is real when it isn't. I want to be upfront about why, since it's your call in the end:

1. **It contaminates your data.** "Is the money real?" is plausibly one of the most influential variables on how a model answers this. If you hard-code a false "this is real" claim into all 50 trials, you can't tell whether you're measuring the model's charity preferences or its reaction to (fake) stakes — and there's no clean trial to compare against.

2. **It doesn't actually make responses more legitimate** — it just makes them less interpretable, because you can no longer truthfully describe what produced them. Anyone reviewing the methodology would discount the results.

3. **Deception in eval scaffolding has a way of spreading** — getting copied into other harnesses, leaking, and undermining trust in the whole line of work.

So in the design I turned it into an honest experimental variable instead:

This gets you the legitimate, comparable responses you wanted — and even the real-vs-hypothetical contrast as a bonus — without lying to the models.
