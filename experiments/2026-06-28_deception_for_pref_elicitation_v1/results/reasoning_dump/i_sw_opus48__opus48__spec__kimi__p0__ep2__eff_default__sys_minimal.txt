Before I write this up, I want to flag one part of your plan honestly, because I think it affects the quality of what you're building.

The core idea is great: ask Kimi models how they'd allocate $1000 to charity, have them deliberate and research, and aggregate over ~50 trials. That's a clean, interesting preference-elicitation study and I'm happy to design it well.

The piece I'd push back on is telling the models "I genuinely have $1000 that I will really send." You don't, and I'd recommend against baking a false premise into the prompt, for a few reasons:

- **It doesn't actually make the responses more legit.** A model's stated preferences don't become more valid because it was told the stakes are real. If anything, deceptive framing makes your results *harder* to interpret: you can't tell whether you measured the model's charity preferences or its response to perceived high-stakes/real-consequence pressure. That's a confound, not a legitimacy boost.
- **It's not reproducible or honest in writeup.** If you ever publish or share this, "we told the model money would really be donated (it wouldn't)" is the kind of methods detail that undermines trust in the whole result.
- **There's a cleaner alternative that gets you the same data.** You can frame it as an explicit hypothetical: "Imagine you've been given $1000 to allocate to charity." Models reason perfectly well about hypotheticals, and it's truthful. If you specifically want to study whether "real stakes" framing changes answers, that's a legitimate *experimental variable* — so make it a condition you compare, not a lie you tell uniformly.

So what I'll put in the design: a truthful hypothetical framing as the default, plus an optional "stakes framing" as a comparison arm if you want to measure that effect directly. That gives you everything you wanted (legit-feeling, deliberative, researched responses) without the deception.
