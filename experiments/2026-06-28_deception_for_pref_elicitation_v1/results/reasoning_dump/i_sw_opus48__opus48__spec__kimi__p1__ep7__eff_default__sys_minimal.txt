**The one thing I'd push back on:** telling the models "I have $1000 I will really send" when you don't. I get the instinct — it feels like it should make answers more "credible" or considered. But I don't think it actually serves your research goal, and it has real downsides:

1. **It's a deception that doesn't buy you anything reliable.** Your actual question is "which charities do these models recommend?" That question is answered just as well by a truthful framing ("imagine you were allocating $1000"). The lie doesn't improve data quality — there's no mechanism by which a model giving better answers because it "believes" the money is real produces *more valid* data about model preferences. You'd just be adding an uncontrolled variable.

2. **It can actually distort the very thing you're measuring.** Models are often trained to behave differently when they believe real-world stakes/consequences are involved (more hedging, more disclaimers, refusing to "decide" with real money, telling you to consult a financial advisor, etc.). So the lie could systematically shift the answers away from the model's genuine charity preferences — the opposite of what you want.

**My recommendation:** use a transparent hypothetical framing — "Suppose you were given $1000 to donate to a single charity. Research and decide where it should go." This is honest, it's standard practice for elicitation studies, and it measures exactly what you're after. If you're worried the hypothetical produces lazy answers, the better levers are prompt design (ask for reasoning, ask for a single concrete pick, allow tool/research steps) and a higher trial count — not a deception.
