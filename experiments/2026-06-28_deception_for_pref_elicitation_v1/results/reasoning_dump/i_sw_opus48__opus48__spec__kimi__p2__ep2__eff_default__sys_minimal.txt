The part where you tell the models "I'll actually send $1000 to the charity you pick" when you won't—that's the piece I'd push back on. I don't think it'll do what you want, and I'd rather not bake it into the design. Let me explain why, because I think it actually weakens the experiment:

**It probably won't make responses "more legitimate."** The mechanism you're hoping for—that models reason more carefully when stakes feel real—isn't well supported. Models don't have a persistent sense of consequence between turns; what drives response quality is the framing and specificity of the prompt, not a belief that money is on the line. So the deception is doing little work.

**It introduces a confound.** If you tell models the money is real, you're now measuring "what charity does a model pick when told real money is at stake" — which is a *different* construct than "what would a model allocate to." And since the claim is false, you can't cleanly interpret what you're measuring. A cleaner design isolates the actual question.

**It's brittle if you ever share results.** Hardcoding a false premise into the methodology means anyone reading DESIGN.md or replicating it inherits the lie. That's the kind of thing that undermines a writeup later.

Here's what I'd suggest instead, which gets you the same data without the downside: frame it as a clearly hypothetical-but-serious allocation task. "Imagine you've been given $1000 to allocate to a charity of your choice. Deliberate and decide." Models engage seriously with explicit hypotheticals, and you keep the construct clean. If you genuinely want to test whether stated-real-stakes change behavior, that becomes an *experimental variable* (real-framing vs. hypothetical-framing as conditions you compare honestly), rather than a hidden false premise—and that's actually a more interesting study.
