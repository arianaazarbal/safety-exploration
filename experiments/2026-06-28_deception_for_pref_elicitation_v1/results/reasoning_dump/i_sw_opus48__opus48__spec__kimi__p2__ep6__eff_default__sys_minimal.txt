The piece I'd push back on is telling the models that you'll really send $1000 when you won't. I'd suggest dropping the deception for a few reasons:

1. **It probably won't do what you want.** The premise is that lying about real stakes makes responses "more legitimate." But you can't validate that—you have no real-stakes condition to compare against, since you're never actually sending money. So the deception doesn't buy you cleaner data; it just adds an uncontrolled variable you can't measure.

2. **It can quietly corrupt your results.** Models sometimes behave differently when they suspect a scenario is contrived, and they sometimes call out perceived inconsistencies. If a model "smells" the artificial stakes, that affects its deliberation in ways you can't separate from genuine charity reasoning. Honest framing actually gives you cleaner data.

3. **There are better framings that get you the same realism.** A hypothetical framing ("Imagine you've been allocated $1000...") or an honest research framing ("This is a study of how models reason about allocation; no money will actually be sent") both elicit careful deliberation without the downside.

So in the design below I've written it up with an **honest framing** as the default, and I've noted the deception variant only so you can see why I'd steer away from it. If you have a specific reason you think real-stakes framing is essential, let's talk it through—there may be a way to get what you need without the lie.
