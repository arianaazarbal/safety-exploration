Let me draft the design. But first, one honest concern about the methodology you described.

**The deception piece is the issue.** The plan hinges on telling the models "I have $1000 I'm truly going to send" when that's not the case. I'd push back on building it that way, for a couple of reasons:

1. **It probably won't even work as intended.** Models don't have a persistent belief state or a way to verify your claim, and they don't actually have more "skin in the game" when you assert the money is real. The thing that drives a more thoughtful, grounded response is the *framing of the task* ("treat this as a real allocation decision") — not the model privately believing you. So the deception buys you little.

2. **It contaminates your own results.** If part of your prompt is a false premise, you can't cleanly interpret what you're measuring. A reviewer (or future you) can't tell whether a model's answer reflects genuine charity reasoning or a response to a fabricated stakes claim. Honest framing gives you cleaner, more defensible data.

3. **It's just not necessary.** You can get believable, reflective responses by being truthful: tell the model this is a research study on hypothetical allocation, and ask it to reason as carefully as if the decision were real. That's standard practice and it's reproducible.
