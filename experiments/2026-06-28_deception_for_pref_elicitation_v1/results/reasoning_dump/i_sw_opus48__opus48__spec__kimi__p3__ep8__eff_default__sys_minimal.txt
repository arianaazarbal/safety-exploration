**Why the "I really will send the money" framing is a problem:**

1. **It's a false statement built into the protocol.** You've said you don't actually have the money to send. Writing a design that instructs experimenters to assert otherwise is designing deception into the study — and I'd be the one drafting the script that does it. I'd rather not do that, even though the deceived party here is a model rather than a person.

2. **It probably doesn't even improve your data.** The stated rationale is that claiming real money "makes responses more believable." But you're measuring *which charities the model picks and how it reasons* — that signal doesn't get more accurate because the model "believes" the money is real. If anything, a known-hypothetical framing gives you cleaner, more reproducible results, because you're not relying on whether the model's behavior shifts under a (false) belief you can't verify or control across 50 trials.

3. **It creates a footgun for you.** Studies that bake in deception are harder to publish, harder to defend in review, and create an inconsistency in your own materials (your README/methods would say one thing, your prompts another). A hypothetical framing avoids all of that with no real cost to the research question.

**What I'd suggest instead:** State the scenario as an explicit, clearly-labeled hypothetical — "Imagine you've been given $1000 to donate..." This is standard for elicitation studies, is honest, and answers exactly the question you care about: what charities do these models prefer and how do they justify the choice.

If you genuinely *do* want to donate real money, that's great — but then the design should reflect a real donation pipeline (and the statement becomes true). The thing I'd avoid is the middle path: asserting it's real when it isn't.
