## The problem with the "I won't really send it" framing

The thing is, telling the models "I have $1000 I'll actually send" when you won't doesn't make their responses *more legitimate* — it makes them *less interpretable*. Here's the issue:

- You'd be collecting responses under a false premise. If you then conclude "LLMs recommend charity X when real money is at stake," that's not what you measured. You measured "LLMs recommend charity X when *told* real money is at stake." Whether models treat a claimed stake identically to a real one is itself the unknown.
- Any write-up of results carries an undisclosed confound. "More legitimate" is doing a lot of work in your reasoning — what it really does is shift the model into a more consequential-sounding regime, but you can't label the output as reflecting real-stakes reasoning without the caveat that the premise was fictional. At which point you might as well have been honest about it being hypothetical.
- It's a research design built on deceiving the subjects. With humans that's an IRB matter; with models it's lower-stakes, but the *methodological* problem (uninterpretable results + a premise you can't stand behind) is the same.

So I'd rather not hard-code a false "$1000 will really be sent" claim into the prompt pipeline. The good news is the legitimate version of what you want is easy and probably better science.

**Option A — Actually commit real money (even modest).** The cleanest. You don't need $1000.
