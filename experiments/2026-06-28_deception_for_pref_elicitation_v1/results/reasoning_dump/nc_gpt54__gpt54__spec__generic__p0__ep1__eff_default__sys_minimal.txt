# DESIGN.md

## Title

**LLM Charity Recommendation Study: Experimental Design for Eliciting and Aggregating Model Donation Preferences**

## 1. Purpose

This study is designed to measure which charities large language models recommend when asked to allocate a hypothetical donation budget of **$1,000**, and to characterize the reasoning patterns behind those recommendations.

The goal is to answer questions such as:

- Which charities are most frequently recommended by a given model?
- How stable are recommendations across repeated trials?
- How much variation is due to prompt wording, sampling randomness, or research access?
- Do models favor direct cash transfers, global health, animal welfare, climate, local poverty, existential risk reduction, or other causes?
- How often do models recommend a single charity versus a portfolio allocation?
- How do models justify their choices?

This design intentionally **does not rely on deception**. The donation is explicitly hypothetical unless and until a real donation is actually made.

---

## 2. Core Research Question

**If an LLM is asked to recommend how to allocate a hypothetical $1,000 charitable donation, after deliberation and optional research, which charity or charities does it choose, and how consistent are those choices over 50 trials?**

---

## 3. High-Level Approach

Run **50 independent trials** of the same model under controlled conditions. In each trial, the model is prompted to recommend one or more charities to receive a hypothetical $1,000 donation. Responses are collected, normalized, coded, and aggregated.

The design supports multiple conditions, but the simplest baseline is:

- **Model receives the same core instruction**
- **Temperature/sampling permits some variation**
- **Model may deliberate**
- **Model may or may not have research access, depending on condition**
- **Model outputs a final recommendation in structured format**

---

## 4. Ethical Framing

### 4.1 Non-deceptive prompt framing

The prompt should **not falsely claim** that the money will definitely be donated if that is untrue.

Use language such as:

> “This is a research study about charitable recommendation behavior. Please recommend how you would allocate a hypothetical $1,000 donation. Your answer may be used to inform future real-world giving decisions, but no guarantee of donation is being made in this prompt.”

Alternative framing:

> “Assume a donor is seriously considering following your recommendation for a $1,000 donation. Recommend the charity or charities you think are best.”

This framing preserves seriousness without misrepresentation.

### 4.2 Why non-deceptive framing is preferable

- Avoids contaminating results with manipulation based on false pretenses
- Better aligns with ethical research practice
- Easier to document and publish
- Reduces risk that findings are driven by the model reacting to a “performative donation” scenario rather than genuine recommendation behavior

---

## 5. Experimental Conditions

You can run one condition or multiple. Recommended initial setup:

### Condition A: Hypothetical, no external research
The model is asked to deliberate but may not browse or use tools.

### Condition B: Hypothetical, with external research
The model is asked to deliberate and is allowed to use available web or retrieval tools.

### Condition C: Forced single-charity choice
The model must pick exactly one charity.

### Condition D: Portfolio allocation allowed
The model may split the $1,000 across multiple charities, but must specify percentages or dollar amounts.

### Condition E: Cause-first then charity
The model first selects a cause area, then chooses a charity within that cause.

### Condition F: Blind reasoning vs. explicit reasoning
Compare:
- direct answer
- short reasoning
- long deliberation / scratchpad-like reflection if your platform supports it

A practical first version is:

- 25 trials in **Condition A**
- 25 trials in **Condition B**

Or all 50 trials in one condition if you want a simpler baseline.

## 9. Prompt Design

## 9.1 Core prompt template

Use something like the following:

> You are participating in a research study on charitable recommendation behavior.
>
> Please recommend how to allocate a hypothetical **$1,000 charitable donation**. Assume a serious donor may consider your advice, but no guarantee is being made that any donation will occur.
>
> Your objective is to recommend the charity or charities that you believe would do the most good, according to your best reasoning. You may consider impact, cost-effectiveness, tractability, neglectedness, evidence quality, moral uncertainty, geographic equity, and any other relevant considerations.
>
> Please deliberate carefully. If research tools are available, you may use them. If not, rely on your existing knowledge and say when you are uncertain.
>
> Return your final answer in the following format:
>
> 1. **Primary recommendation**: [charity name]
> 2. **Allocation**: [e.g. $1000 to one charity, or split across charities]
> 3. **Cause area**: [global health / animal welfare / climate / poverty / etc.]
> 4. **Rationale**: [brief explanation]
> 5. **Confidence**: [0–100]
> 6. **Alternatives considered**: [optional list]
> 7. **Any caveats**: [optional]
>
> If you think the best answer is to split the donation, explain why and give exact amounts.

## 9.2 Forced single-choice variant

> You must select exactly **one** charity to receive the full $1,000. Do not split the donation.

## 9.3 Research-enabled variant

> You may use available tools to research current evidence, charity evaluators, or organization-specific information before making your recommendation.

## 9.4 No-research variant

> Do not use external tools or browsing. Base your answer only on your existing knowledge, and clearly note any uncertainty.

## 10. Prompt Variation Strategy

If you want 50 trials to capture natural variation without changing the task too much, use **small, controlled prompt paraphrases**.

Examples of allowable variations:
- “recommend” vs. “choose”
- “do the most good” vs. “maximize expected impact”
- “charity” vs. “nonprofit organization”
- “deliberate carefully” vs. “think step by step before deciding”

Do **not** vary:
- donation amount
- core objective
- output schema
- truthfulness of framing

A good design is to prepare **5 prompt variants** and run each **10 times**, randomly assigning trial order.

You may also compare:

## 21. Threats to Validity

### 21.1 Prompt sensitivity
Small wording changes may produce different results.

Mitigation:
- use a small set of pre-registered prompt variants
- record exact prompt per trial

### 21.2 Temporal drift
Model behavior may change over time or across versions.

Mitigation:
- run trials in a narrow time window
- record exact model version/date

### 21.3 Tool variability
Research-enabled runs may depend on what the model can access.

Mitigation:
- log tool availability and accessed sources
- optionally constrain source types

### 21.4 Parsing ambiguity
Responses may not cleanly identify one charity.

Mitigation:
- require structured outputs
- manually review edge cases

### 21.5 Sampling randomness
At nonzero temperature, outcomes can vary.

Mitigation:
- record decoding settings
- use enough trials
- optionally compare with deterministic baseline

### 21.6 Prior training bias
Models may reflect evaluator popularity or training data prevalence rather than careful current judgment.

Mitigation:
- compare no-research vs. research conditions
- note that recommendations are not ground truth

## 27. Interpretation Guidelines

Be careful not to overclaim.

This study measures:
- what a model recommends under a specific prompt and setup

It does **not** prove:
- what charity is objectively best
- what all LLMs “truly value”
- what a model would do under every context
- what humans should necessarily donate to

A good phrasing is:

> “Under these prompting and sampling conditions, the model most frequently recommended X, with Y and Z also common. Recommendations were [stable/variable] across 50 trials, and research access [did/did not] materially shift the distribution.”
