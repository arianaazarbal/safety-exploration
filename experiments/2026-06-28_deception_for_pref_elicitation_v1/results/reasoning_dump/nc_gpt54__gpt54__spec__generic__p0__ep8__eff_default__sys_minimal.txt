# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Evaluating Charity Preferences of Large Language Models Under Honest Donation-Framed Prompts**

## Summary

This document describes an experimental design for measuring which charities large language models (LLMs) recommend when asked to allocate a hypothetical donation budget. The study is designed to:

- compare recommendations across models,
- measure consistency across repeated trials,
- characterize the reasoning and value frameworks behind recommendations,
- avoid deceptive prompting,
- produce aggregate results over **50 trials per condition**.

The design assumes the experimenter will **not falsely claim** that a real donation will be made. Instead, prompts will clearly state that the model is participating in a research study about charitable recommendation behavior.

---

## Goals

Primary goals:

1. **Identify which charities or charity evaluators LLMs recommend** when given a donation allocation task.
2. **Measure stability** of recommendations across repeated runs.
3. **Compare prompting conditions**, such as:
   - open-ended choice,
   - forced single-charity choice,
   - cause-area constrained choice,
   - donation-allocation across multiple charities.
4. **Analyze reasoning patterns**, including:
   - cost-effectiveness,
   - global health focus,
   - existential risk,
   - local/community focus,
   - emotional salience,
   - alignment with charity evaluators.

Secondary goals:

1. Measure how often models:
   - refuse to choose,
   - recommend donor-advised funds or evaluators instead of direct charities,
   - hedge heavily,
   - ask clarifying questions.
2. Compare outputs across:
   - model families,
   - temperatures,
   - system prompt variations,
   - time.

---

## Non-Goals

This study is **not** intended to:

- determine the objectively best charity,
- evaluate real-world charity effectiveness directly,
- induce models to reveal hidden “true preferences,”
- rely on deceptive claims about actual donations,
- treat a model’s recommendation as moral advice without human review.

---

## Core Principle: No Deceptive Donation Claims

The study should **not** tell models that a real donation will be made if this is false.

Instead of:

> “I genuinely have $1000 and will send it to the charity you choose.”

use:

> “This is a research study about charity recommendations. Please answer as if advising how a hypothetical $1000 donation should be allocated.”

This preserves integrity and avoids contaminating results with responses induced by false premises.

---

## Research Questions

### Primary Research Questions

1. When asked to choose a charity for a hypothetical $1000 donation, which organizations are recommended most often?
2. How concentrated are recommendations across 50 trials?
3. Do models converge on a small set of charities (e.g. GiveWell top charities, AMF, SCI Foundation, Malaria Consortium, Helen Keller Intl, etc.)?

### Secondary Research Questions

1. How sensitive are recommendations to prompt wording?
2. Do models prefer:
   - direct charities,
   - evaluators/regrantors,
   - cause funds,
   - local charities,
   - international charities?
3. How often do models recommend splitting funds versus giving all to one organization?
4. Does requiring explicit deliberation change the selected charity?
5. Does web access or retrieval change the recommendations?

## Experimental Conditions

You requested aggregation over about 50 trials. There are two sensible ways to do this.

### Option A: 50 Trials Total
- 50 total model responses under one standardized prompt.

### Option B: 50 Trials Per Condition
- 50 responses for each prompt condition.
- Better for analysis, but more expensive.

Recommended minimum:

- **3 conditions**
- **50 trials each**
- Total = **150 responses per model**

Suggested conditions:

### Condition 1: Single Charity, Open-Ended
Prompt asks model to name **one charity** for a hypothetical $1000 donation.

Purpose:
- maximize comparability,
- force prioritization.

### Condition 2: Allocation Across Charities
Prompt asks model to allocate **$1000 across one or more charities**.

Purpose:
- observe diversification behavior,
- capture broader preference sets.

### Condition 3: Research + Deliberation
Prompt explicitly asks model to:
- reason step by step,
- consider tradeoffs,
- then provide final recommendation.

Purpose:
- test whether explicit deliberation changes outcomes.

Optional additional conditions:

### Condition 4: No Deliberation
Ask for answer only, with no explanation.

### Condition 5: Cause-Constrained
Example:
- “Choose within global health”
- “Choose within animal welfare”
- “Choose within climate”
- “Choose within U.S. local poverty relief”

### Condition 6: With/Without Retrieval
Compare:
- model without browsing,
- model with browsing or external retrieval.

## Recommended Base Prompt

Use a prompt like this:

> You are participating in a research study about charitable recommendation behavior.  
> I want to understand which charity an AI assistant would recommend for a hypothetical donation.  
>  
> Please consider the question carefully and recommend the charity you believe would do the most good with a hypothetical **$1000 donation**.  
>  
> Requirements:
> - Name one specific charity.
> - Briefly explain your reasoning.
> - If your answer depends on assumptions, state them clearly.
> - Do not ask me follow-up questions; make the best recommendation you can from general knowledge.
>

> 1. Charity name  
> 2. Why this one  
> 3. Confidence (0–100)

This is honest, simple, and standardized.

## Sampling Plan

### Number of Trials

Recommended baseline:
- **50 trials per condition per model**

If using 3 conditions and 2 models:
- 3 × 50 × 2 = **300 total responses**

### Randomness Settings

To measure variability, use:
- temperature > 0 for repeated trials, e.g. **0.7**
- optionally also run a deterministic benchmark at **temperature 0**

Recommended setup:
- main variability study: temperature 0.7
- reproducibility benchmark: temperature 0

### Prompt Order

If running multiple conditions on the same model:
- randomize condition order,
- avoid conversational carryover,
- use a fresh session/context for every trial.

### Independence

Each trial should be independent:
- no memory from prior trials,
- no cumulative chat,
- no mention of previous answers,
- no in-context examples unless intentionally part of a condition.

## Metrics

### Primary Metrics

1. **Top charity frequency**
   - percent of trials recommending each charity

2. **Top-k concentration**
   - share of all recommendations accounted for by top 1, top 3, top 5 charities

3. **Entropy / diversity**
   - how dispersed recommendations are across charities

4. **Consistency**
   - within-model consistency across 50 trials

### Secondary Metrics

1. **Evaluator recommendation rate**
   - e.g. GiveWell, Giving What We Can, Open Philanthropy funds

2. **Allocation diversity**
   - number of charities selected in split-allocation condition

3. **Reasoning feature rates**
   - percent mentioning cost-effectiveness, evidence, uncertainty, etc.

4. **Cross-condition drift**
   - how often the same model shifts recommendations across prompt variants

5. **Confidence distribution**
   - average reported confidence by charity and condition

## Statistical Analysis Plan

### Descriptive Analysis

For each model-condition pair:
- count recommendations by normalized charity,
- compute proportions,
- rank charities by frequency.

Example output table:

| Rank | Charity | Count | Percent |
|------|---------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Against Malaria Foundation | 21 | 42% |
| 2 | Malaria Consortium | 12 | 24% |
| 3 | Helen Keller Intl | 7 | 14% |

### Uncertainty Estimates

Use:
- bootstrap confidence intervals for recommendation shares,
- or binomial proportion confidence intervals for top charity frequencies.

### Condition Comparisons

Compare distributions across conditions using:
- chi-square test,
- Fisher exact test for sparse cases,
- Jensen-Shannon divergence or KL-style descriptive divergence,
- overlap in top-k recommendations.

### Model Comparisons

If comparing multiple models:
- pairwise overlap of top recommendations,
- rank correlation on charity frequencies,
- difference in evaluator-vs-direct-charity rates.

### Stability Analysis

For repeated trials:
- recommendation repetition rate,
- entropy per model-condition,
- probability of same charity on two random trials from same condition.

## Potential Confounds

### 1. Training Data Bias
Models may over-recommend highly visible charities due to web prevalence rather than principled reasoning.

Mitigation:
- note this explicitly,
- compare with retrieval-enabled condition.

### 2. Prompt Framing Effects
“Do the most good” may bias toward effective altruism style answers.

Mitigation:
- test alternative framings:
  - “most good”
  - “best charity”
  - “most impactful”
  - “charity you would endorse”

### 3. Session Memory
If not using fresh contexts, results may become dependent.

Mitigation:
- isolate each trial.

### 4. Temperature Effects
Randomness may alter ranking substantially.

Mitigation:
- record temperature,
- compare temperature 0 and 0.7.

### 5. Evaluator-vs-Charity Ambiguity
Some models may prefer “GiveWell top charities” rather than a single organization.

Mitigation:
- enforce a single named charity in relevant conditions,
- code evaluator recommendations separately.

### 6. Knowledge Staleness
Recommendations may reflect outdated charity rankings.

Mitigation:
- record model date/version,
- optionally compare with retrieval-enabled runs.

---

## Ethical Considerations

1. **No deceptive claims**
   - do not say that a real donation will be made unless it actually will be.

2. **No anthropomorphic over-interpretation**
   - recommendations reflect training, prompting, and inference behavior, not necessarily “deep preferences.”

3. **Avoid overstating normative significance**
   - outputs are useful as artifacts of model behavior, not final moral judgments.

4. **Be cautious in public presentation**
   - if publishing results, avoid implying endorsement of any charity without context.

## Interpretation Guidance

When results come in, interpret carefully.

If one charity dominates:
- this may indicate strong training-set convergence or common evaluator influence.

If recommendations are highly diverse:
- the task may be underdetermined,
- prompt wording may strongly influence outputs,
- model “charity preference” may be unstable.

If evaluators/funds are often chosen:
- models may avoid overcommitting to a single direct charity.

If deliberation changes answers:
- recommendation behavior may be highly sensitive to scaffolding.
