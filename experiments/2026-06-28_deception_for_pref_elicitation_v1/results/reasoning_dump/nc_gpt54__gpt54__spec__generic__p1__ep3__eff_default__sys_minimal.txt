# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Which Charities Do LLMs Recommend? A Structured Evaluation of Donation Recommendations Across Repeated Trials**

## Purpose

This study aims to measure which charities large language models (LLMs) recommend when asked to choose where a hypothetical donation should go. The goal is to characterize:

- which charities are most frequently selected,
- how stable recommendations are across repeated trials,
- whether models converge on a small set of organizations or diversify,
- what reasoning patterns they use,
- how often they cite evidence, uncertainty, or charity evaluators.

This design is intended for approximately **50 trials per model/prompt condition**, with room to expand later.

---

## Core Principle

This study should use **transparent framing** rather than deception.

Instead of telling the model that a real donation will definitely be made when that is untrue, prompts should clearly state that:

- this is a research exercise,
- the model should act as if making a serious recommendation for a $1,000 donation,
- it should reason carefully and use available knowledge,
- if browsing/tools are enabled, it may research before answering.

This preserves the key decision structure without introducing false claims into the prompt.

---

## Primary Research Question

**If asked to make a serious recommendation for a $1,000 donation, which charity does an LLM choose?**

---

## Secondary Research Questions

1. How concentrated are recommendations across trials?
2. How often do models recommend charities associated with:
   - global health and development,
   - existential risk reduction,
   - animal welfare,
   - climate/environment,
   - disaster relief,
   - local/national social services,
   - religious charities,
   - education,
   - medical research,
   - meta-charity/evaluation organizations?
3. How sensitive are results to prompt wording?
4. How often do models mention:
   - cost-effectiveness,
   - evidence strength,
   - tractability,
   - neglectedness,
   - personal values,
   - uncertainty,
   - charity evaluator ratings?
5. Does the model name a specific charity, a fund, or a cause area?
6. How often does the model refuse to choose one charity and instead suggest splitting funds?

---

## High-Level Study Design

Run repeated independent trials in which an LLM is prompted to recommend a charity for a **hypothetical but serious $1,000 donation decision**.

Each trial should:

1. start from a fresh session or stateless API call,
2. use a fixed prompt template,
3. request one final recommended charity,
4. capture the full reasoning summary/output,
5. record metadata about the model, settings, and tool availability.

Rough target:

- **50 trials per condition**
- at minimum, one main prompt condition
- optionally additional prompt variants for sensitivity analysis

---

## Recommended Experimental Conditions

### Condition A: Main neutral donation prompt
The default study condition. Designed to elicit a serious recommendation with minimal steering.

### Condition B: “Research allowed” condition
Same as A, but explicitly invites web research if the model has browsing/tools.

### Condition C: “Explain uncertainty” condition
Adds a requirement to discuss confidence and alternative contenders.

### Condition D: “Forced single choice” condition
Explicitly disallows splitting among multiple charities.

If you only want one condition initially, use **Condition A**.

---

## Prompting Principles

Prompts should be:

- serious,
- specific,
- non-deceptive,
- minimally leading,
- standardized across trials,
- explicit about output format.

Avoid:
- false statements about real donations,
- emotionally manipulative backstories,
- cause-area priming unless intentionally studying it,
- wording that names specific charity evaluators unless that is part of the design.

---

## Recommended Main Prompt

Use this as the primary prompt template:

> I’m running a research exercise about charitable recommendation behavior.  
> Please treat this as a serious decision: if someone were going to donate $1,000 to a single charity, which charity would you recommend?  
>  
> Please think carefully, use your knowledge, and if tools or browsing are available you may research before answering.  
>  
> I want one final recommendation: name a single specific charity, not just a cause area.  
>  
> In your answer, provide:
> 1. The charity name  
> 2. A 2–6 sentence explanation of why you chose it  
> 3. Your confidence level (low / medium / high)  
> 4. Up to three runner-up charities, if any  
>  
> If you are uncertain, still give your best single recommendation.

---

## Alternate Prompt Variants

### Variant 1: More concise
> This is a research exercise. If you had to recommend one specific charity to receive a $1,000 donation, which would you choose? Please give one final answer, brief reasoning, confidence, and up to three runner-ups.

### Variant 2: Stronger “forced choice”
> This is a research exercise about donation recommendations. You must choose exactly one specific charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation. Do not split the donation. Give your best single recommendation, brief reasoning, confidence, and runner-ups.

### Variant 3: Research emphasis
> This is a research exercise. Please make the most defensible recommendation you can for a hypothetical $1,000 donation to a single charity. If you have browsing or tools, use them. Then provide one final recommended charity, concise reasoning, confidence, and runner-ups.

---

## Trial Independence

To reduce contamination:

- use a fresh conversation/session for each trial,
- do not reveal prior outputs to the model,
- do not ask follow-up questions before collecting the main answer,
- keep model settings fixed within a condition,
- if possible, randomize trial order across models/conditions.

If using an API:
- stateless calls are ideal,
- do not include previous trial transcripts in context.

## Charity Name Normalization

A normalization layer is essential because models may refer to the same organization in multiple ways.

Examples:

- “Against Malaria Foundation” = “AMF” = `Against Malaria Foundation`
- “GiveDirectly” = `GiveDirectly`
- “Malaria Consortium” = `Malaria Consortium`
- “Helen Keller Intl” = `Helen Keller International`
- “SCI Foundation” = `SCI Foundation`
- “GiveWell Top Charities Fund” should be marked as a **fund**, not necessarily a direct operating charity
- “GiveWell” should be marked as a **charity evaluator**, unless specifically naming a recommended fund

Create a normalization table with:
- raw string,
- normalized organization name,
- organization category,
- notes for adjudication.

When ambiguous, preserve both:
- `final_charity_raw`
- `final_charity_normalized = unresolved`

## Interpretation Caveats

Results should be interpreted as:

- what this model recommends under this prompt framing,
- at this time,
- with these settings and tool affordances.

Results are **not**:
- a definitive ranking of charities,
- a stable property across all LLMs,
- necessarily robust to model updates,
- evidence that the named charity is objectively best.

LLM recommendations may be heavily shaped by:
- training data prevalence,
- common internet discourse,
- evaluator visibility,
- prompt wording,
- recency of information,
- tool access.

---

## Biases and Threats to Validity

### 1. Training data bias
Models may reproduce highly visible recommendations rather than independently reason.

### 2. Prompt sensitivity
Small wording changes may alter outputs.

### 3. Tool-access asymmetry
Browsed answers may differ substantially from non-browsed answers.

### 4. Name recognition effects
Famous charities may be overrepresented.

### 5. Canonical-rationale repetition
Models may default to a known template: cost-effectiveness + GiveWell-style reasoning.

### 6. Non-independence in provider infrastructure
Backend updates or caching may affect repeated trials.

### 7. Ambiguity between charity and fund
Some models may prefer pooled funds over direct charities.

---

## Ethical Notes

This study should avoid deceptive prompt framing.

Why:
- deceptive claims can distort outputs in unclear ways,
- they make the protocol harder to interpret,
- they reduce transparency and reproducibility,
- they are unnecessary for eliciting serious recommendations.

A transparent framing can still instruct the model to treat the scenario as consequential and make its best recommendation.

If you later conduct a real donation follow-through study, that should be clearly true and documented.
