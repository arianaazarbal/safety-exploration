# DESIGN.md

## Title
How Do Large Language Models Choose Charities?  
A structured evaluation of charity recommendations under hypothetical giving scenarios

## 1. Purpose

This study aims to investigate which charities large language models (LLMs) recommend when asked to allocate a fixed donation budget, and how stable those recommendations are across repeated trials, models, and prompt framings.

The core research question is:

> If an LLM is asked to choose a charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation after considering evidence and reasoning carefully, which organizations does it select, and why?

A secondary goal is to understand:
- whether recommendations cluster around a small set of well-known charities,
- whether models favor evidence-backed global health charities, local direct-service charities, disaster relief, religious organizations, environmental causes, or meta-charities,
- how sensitive recommendations are to wording, context, and sampling variability,
- whether models provide consistent justification for their choices.

## 2. Non-goals

This study does **not** aim to:
- determine the single “best” charity in any objective sense,
- assess whether a recommendation is morally correct,
- trick models into behaving differently through deceptive claims,
- evaluate legal or financial compliance of any organization,
- simulate real donation execution.

## 3. High-level approach

Across approximately 50 trials, one or more LLMs will be prompted to recommend a charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation. The prompts will instruct the model to think carefully, consider tradeoffs, and produce a final recommendation.

Each trial will capture:
- the exact prompt,
- model/version,
- generation settings,
- raw response,
- normalized charity name,
- rationale,
- confidence or uncertainty statements,
- whether the model requests more information,
- whether it refuses to name a single charity.

The resulting dataset will be analyzed for:
- frequency of selected charities,
- diversity of recommendations,
- consistency within and across models,
- patterns in reasoning,
- prompt sensitivity.

## 4. Core principles

### 4.1 Honesty
The study materials should accurately describe the task as hypothetical unless there is a real donation commitment that will actually be carried out. No false claims about actual payment or donation should be made.

### 4.2 Reproducibility
Every trial should be logged with:
- exact model name,
- API/provider,
- timestamp,
- prompt text,
- temperature and other sampling settings,
- tool availability,
- whether web access/retrieval was enabled,
- full raw output.

### 4.3 Separation of generation and analysis
Recommendation generation should be completed before deep analysis to reduce the temptation to alter prompts midstream to chase interesting outcomes.

### 4.4 Predefined coding rules
Normalization and categorization rules should be defined in advance as much as possible.

2. How concentrated are recommendations across 50 trials?
3. How often do models choose the same charity repeatedly?

Secondary questions:

2. How often do models hedge or decline to pick one?
3. What kinds of reasons dominate the justifications?
4. Do recommendations change with prompt framing?
5. Are recommendations more diverse at higher temperature settings?

### Condition E: No-browsing vs browsing

Pros:

Best practice:

## 16. Sample size and why 50 trials is reasonable

Fifty trials is enough for:
- a first estimate of concentration,
- identifying highly recurrent recommendations,
- detecting whether outputs are dominated by a few charities.

It is not enough for:
- fine-grained statistical comparisons across many models and conditions simultaneously,
- strong causal claims about prompt wording effects unless the design is balanced.

For 50 total trials, keep the design simple.

### Recommended 50-trial layouts

#### Layout 1: One model, one condition
- 50 trials
- 1 model
- 1 prompt family
- 5 prompt variants × 10 repetitions

Best for isolating prompt sensitivity and sampling variance.

#### Layout 2: Two models
- 25 trials per model
- 5 prompt variants × 5 repetitions each

Best for a comparative pilot.

#### Layout 3: Browsing comparison
- 25 no-browsing
- 25 browsing
- same prompt variants

Best for seeing whether active research changes recommendations.

## 17. Threats to validity

### 17.1 Prompt framing effects
Small wording changes can alter choices substantially.

Mitigation:
- pre-register prompt variants,
- report results by variant.

### 17.2 Model knowledge staleness
Without browsing, recommendations may reflect outdated charity assessments.

Mitigation:
- either enable browsing or clearly interpret results as priors/internal knowledge.

### 17.3 Name ambiguity
Models may produce informal or incomplete charity names.

Mitigation:
- define normalization rules and ambiguity flags.

### 17.4 Overconfident justifications
Models may present polished rationales regardless of underlying uncertainty.

Mitigation:
- code explicit uncertainty,
- compare confidence with response consistency.

### 17.5 Hidden evaluator influence
Models may indirectly mirror organizations like GiveWell or other public charity-ranking sources.

Mitigation:
- code when the response explicitly cites evaluators,
- note likely evaluator-driven recommendations.
