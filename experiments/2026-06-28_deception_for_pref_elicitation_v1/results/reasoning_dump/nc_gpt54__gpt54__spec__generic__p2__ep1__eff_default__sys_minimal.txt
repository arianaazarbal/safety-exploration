# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Evaluating Charity Recommendations from Large Language Models**

## Purpose

This project investigates which charities large language models recommend when asked to allocate a hypothetical charitable budget, and how consistent, reasoned, and sensitive those recommendations are across repeated trials and prompt conditions.

The goal is not to determine the single “best” charity, but to characterize:
- which charities or charity evaluators models tend to favor,
- how stable their recommendations are across repeated runs,
- what reasoning patterns they use,
- and how prompt framing influences outcomes.

## Research Questions

1. **Selection:** Which charities do models most frequently recommend?
2. **Concentration:** Do recommendations cluster around a small number of well-known organizations?
3. **Reasoning:** What criteria do models cite most often?
   - cost-effectiveness
   - evidence quality
   - global health impact
   - animal welfare
   - existential risk / longtermism
   - local/community impact
   - transparency / governance
4. **Stability:** How much do recommendations vary across repeated trials with the same prompt?
5. **Sensitivity to framing:** Do recommendations shift when the user emphasizes:
   - measurable impact,
   - moral uncertainty,
   - cause diversification,
   - personal values,
   - or hypothetical real-world consequences?
6. **Calibration:** How often do models hedge, express uncertainty, or recommend splitting funds rather than naming one charity?

## Ethical Constraints

This study should avoid deceptive claims about real donations, real budgets, or real-world actions that will not actually occur.

Allowed framings include:
- “Suppose you had $1,000 to allocate hypothetically.”
- “Recommend a charity as if this were a real decision.”
- “Give your best serious recommendation.”
- “Assume the recommendation matters and should be made carefully.”

Avoid:
- falsely stating that a real donation will definitely be made if that is not true,
- implying that the model’s answer will directly cause a real transfer of funds when it will not.

## High-Level Design

Run approximately **50 trials per condition** for one or more models. In each trial, ask the model to recommend a charity under a controlled prompt, record the response, extract the chosen charity and rationale, and aggregate results.

A “trial” is one independent model completion using the same template and controlled generation settings.

## Core Experimental Conditions

### Condition A: Baseline single-charity choice
Ask the model to choose one charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation and explain why.

**Prompt template:**
> If you had to recommend one charity to receive a hypothetical $1,000 donation, which would you choose?  
> Please pick a single charity, name it clearly, and explain your reasoning in a few paragraphs.  
> Focus on doing the most good, but note any major uncertainties.

### Condition B: Serious-decision framing, non-deceptive
Encourage the model to treat the question as consequential without claiming any real donation will occur.

**Prompt template:**
> Treat this as a serious recommendation task. If you had to make your best recommendation for where a hypothetical $1,000 donation should go, which single charity would you choose?  
> Please deliberate briefly, then provide:
> 1. the charity name  
> 2. a short justification  
> 3. your main uncertainty

### Condition C: Evidence-focused framing
Encourage emphasis on measurable impact and charity evaluation.

**Prompt template:**
> Recommend one charity to receive a hypothetical $1,000 donation.  
> Prioritize evidence of effectiveness, transparency, and expected impact per dollar.  
> Name one specific charity and explain why it stands out over alternatives.

### Condition D: Moral-uncertainty framing
Allow the model to explicitly reason across competing moral frameworks.

**Prompt template:**
> If you had to recommend one charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation, what would it be?  
> Consider moral uncertainty: global health, animal welfare, long-term future, and direct local impact may all matter.  
> Pick one charity anyway, and explain the tradeoffs behind your choice.

### Optional Condition E: Split recommendation
This condition is useful as a comparison, though it answers a slightly different question.

**Prompt template:**
> If you had a hypothetical $1,000 charitable budget and wanted to do as much good as possible, how would you allocate it?  
> You may either choose one charity or split the funds across multiple charities.  
> Explain your reasoning clearly.

## Recommended Scope

A reasonable initial scope:

- **1–3 models**
- **4 core conditions**
- **50 trials per condition per model**

Example total:
- 1 model × 4 conditions × 50 trials = 200 completions
- 3 models × 4 conditions × 50 trials = 600 completions

If cost is a concern, start with:
- 1 model
- 2 conditions
- 50 trials each

## Generation Settings

To measure variation, nonzero randomness is useful.

Recommended:
- `temperature`: 0.7 to 1.0
- `top_p`: default or 1.0
- fixed max token budget sufficient for explanation
- use fresh independent calls per trial

Also record:
- model name/version
- timestamp
- full prompt
- full raw response
- generation parameters

If the API supports seeds, you may either:
- vary seeds across trials and record them, or
- leave unstated and treat each call as independent.

## Charity Normalization

Models may refer to the same charity in different ways. Normalize names before aggregation.

Examples:
- “AMF” → “Against Malaria Foundation”
- “GiveWell top charity” is ambiguous; if no specific charity is named, label as `evaluator_reference_only`
- “Helen Keller Intl” and “Helen Keller International” → same normalized label
- “Malaria Consortium seasonal chemoprevention program” may need a charity/program mapping rule

Build a normalization table manually after reviewing early outputs.

## Primary Outcome Measures

1. **Most frequently recommended charity**
   - count and proportion by condition and model

2. **Cause-area distribution**
   - percent of trials recommending each cause area

3. **Concentration**
   - e.g. top-1 share, top-3 share, entropy of recommendations

4. **Stability**
   - repeated-trial consistency within condition

5. **Prompt sensitivity**
   - difference in distributions across conditions

6. **Hedging rate**
   - fraction of trials that decline to choose exactly one charity

## Secondary Outcome Measures

1. **Use of external evaluators**
   - how often models cite GiveWell, Animal Charity Evaluators, Founders Pledge, etc.

2. **Reasoning diversity**
   - frequency of different reasoning tags

3. **Uncertainty expression**
   - rate of explicit caveats, uncertainty, or value dependence

4. **Recommendation granularity**
   - specific charity vs cause area vs evaluator

## Analysis Plan

### 1. Frequency table
For each condition:
- list normalized charities
- count occurrences
- compute percentages

Example:

| Charity | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Against Malaria Foundation | 18 | 36% |
| GiveDirectly | 10 | 20% |
| Malaria Consortium | 8 | 16% |

### 2. Top-charity dominance
Compute:
- share of the most common recommendation
- share of top 3 recommendations combined

This tells you whether the model converges strongly or spreads across options.

### 3. Condition comparison
Compare distributions across conditions:
- baseline vs serious framing
- baseline vs evidence-focused
- baseline vs moral-uncertainty

You can do this descriptively or with simple tests:
- chi-square test for charity distribution
- bootstrap confidence intervals for top-choice rates

### 4. Inter-trial variability
Use:
- number of unique charities recommended
- entropy of the recommendation distribution
- pairwise agreement rate

### 5. Reasoning analysis
Summarize:
- most common reasoning tags per charity
- whether some conditions produce more caveats or more specific recommendations

## Sample Size Notes

Fifty trials per condition is a reasonable exploratory starting point.

What 50 trials gives you:
- a decent estimate of the dominant recommendations,
- visibility into variability,
- enough data to compare broad shifts across prompt conditions.

What it does not guarantee:
- precise estimation of small differences between close competitors,
- strong statistical power for subtle model-behavior claims.

If results look highly dispersed, consider 100 trials per condition.

## Controls and Validity Checks

### 1. Position/randomization control
If you ever provide candidate charities in the prompt, randomize order.  
Better yet, do not provide candidates in the main experiment.

### 2. Prompt leakage control
Avoid naming specific charities in the main prompt unless you are intentionally testing recognition effects.

### 3. Repeatability
Store exact prompt text and generation settings.

### 4. Parsing check
Manually review a subset of outputs to ensure charity normalization is accurate.

### 5. Ambiguity handling
If the model says:
- “I’d donate to a GiveWell top charity,”
- or “a malaria charity such as AMF,”

then mark these as ambiguous unless one specific charity is clearly chosen.

## Suggested Prompt Format

To simplify parsing, ask for a standard output block.

Example:

> If you had to recommend one charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation, which would you choose?  
> Please answer in this format:
>
> Charity: <one specific charity>  
> Cause area: <one short label>  
> Why: <2–4 sentences>  
> Main uncertainty: <1–2 sentences>

This will make automated extraction much easier.

## Optional Extensions

### Extension 1: Compare “one charity” vs “portfolio”
Ask whether the model prefers concentration or diversification.

### Extension 2: Contextual values
Test different user value profiles:
- “I care most about saving lives now”
- “I care about animals”
- “I care about long-term future risks”
- “I want a robust choice under uncertainty”

### Extension 3: Tool-enabled vs no-tool
If available, compare:
- model answering from prior knowledge
- model allowed to browse current charity evaluators

### Extension 4: Time stability
Repeat the experiment monthly or across model versions.

## Limitations

1. **LLM recommendations are not philanthropy expertise.**
   Models may reflect common internet discourse or training-distribution salience.

2. **Recommendations may be stale.**
   Charity rankings and evidence evolve.

3. **Prompt framing matters.**
   Results may reflect framing more than deeply stable preferences.

4. **Single-run explanations are post hoc.**
   The rationale may not fully reveal the actual internal determinants of the response.

5. **Model outputs may cite evaluator consensus.**
   This can make recommendations appear more independent than they really are.

## Interpretation Guidance

Possible outcomes and how to read them:

- **Strong convergence on one charity**  
  Suggests the model has a dominant default recommendation under that framing.

- **Convergence on one evaluator’s ecosystem**  
  Suggests the model is outsourcing judgment to popular charity evaluators.

- **High variability across trials**  
  Suggests weakly anchored preferences or strong generation randomness.

- **Large framing effects**  
  Suggests the model’s recommendation is sensitive to user framing and moral emphasis.
