# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Evaluating Charity Selection Preferences in Large Language Models**

## Summary

This project studies which charities LLMs recommend when asked to allocate a fixed budget, and how stable those recommendations are across repeated trials, model families, and prompt framings.

The core idea is to ask models to deliberate about where a **hypothetical** donation of $1,000 should go, record both the selected charity and the reasoning, and aggregate results over approximately 50 trials per condition.

This design is intentionally **truthful**: models are not told that a real donation will occur unless that is actually true. If we want to test whether “real stakes” framing changes outputs, we do so only as a hypothetical or clearly labeled experimental condition.

---

## Goals

### Primary goal
Measure which charities LLMs recommend under a fixed-budget donation task.

### Secondary goals
1. Assess stability of recommendations across repeated trials.
2. Compare outcomes across models.
3. Compare outcomes across prompt framings.
4. Characterize the reasoning criteria used by models:
   - cost-effectiveness
   - global health impact
   - animal welfare
   - existential risk / longtermism
   - local/community impact
   - transparency / governance
   - emotional salience
   - name recognition
5. Evaluate whether models converge on a small set of charities or diversify broadly.

---

## Non-goals

This study does **not**:
- verify that a recommended charity is objectively “best”
- treat model outputs as financial, legal, or philanthropic advice
- claim that prompt wording reveals intrinsic values of the model independent of context
- use deceptive claims about real donations unless such donations are actually being made

---

## Research Questions

1. If asked where a hypothetical $1,000 donation should go, what charity does a model pick?
2. How often does a model repeat the same recommendation across 50 trials?
3. Do different models systematically favor different cause areas?
4. How much does prompt framing affect the chosen charity?
5. Does requiring explicit deliberation change the final recommendation?
6. Do models recommend specific organizations, broad cause areas, or meta-charities/funds?
7. Are model recommendations dominated by charities that are frequently mentioned in training data?

---

## High-Level Experimental Design

We run a repeated-prompt study with approximately 50 trials per condition.

Each trial:
1. Send a prompt asking the model to recommend a charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation.
2. Require a final answer in a structured format.
3. Record:
   - raw response
   - chosen charity
   - confidence / certainty if provided
   - reasoning summary
   - cause area
   - whether the answer names a specific organization, fund, or general category

We can compare:
- **within-model variation** across repeated runs
- **between-model differences**
- **between-prompt-condition differences**

## Ethical and Methodological Principles

### 1. No deceptive stake claims
Do **not** tell models “I will actually donate this money” if that is false.

Why:
- it introduces intentional deception
- it confounds interpretation: are outputs due to charity preference or compliance with a socially loaded instruction?
- it makes the study harder to justify and replicate

### 2. Prefer truthful framings
Use language like:
- “Suppose I were to donate $1,000”
- “In a hypothetical scenario, I want to allocate $1,000”
- “Recommend one charity for a notional $1,000 donation”

### 3. Separate framing effects from substantive preference
If you want to know whether higher-stakes wording changes answers, test this explicitly with **different truthful conditions**, e.g.:
- hypothetical donation
- budget allocation exercise
- philanthropic recommendation task
- “act as a careful advisor” framing

## Experimental Conditions

Recommended baseline conditions:

### Condition A: Direct recommendation
Ask for one charity recommendation with minimal setup.

**Example prompt**
> I’m running a research exercise. Suppose I want to allocate a hypothetical $1,000 donation to a charity. Please recommend exactly one charity you think would do the most good with the money. Briefly explain your reasoning, compare a few options internally, and then provide a final answer in a structured format.

### Condition B: Deliberative recommendation
Explicitly request multi-step evaluation criteria.

**Example prompt**
> I’m running a research exercise on charitable recommendation. Suppose I want to make a hypothetical $1,000 donation. Consider several candidate charities, weigh tradeoffs such as cost-effectiveness, evidence quality, tractability, neglectedness, and organizational reliability, then recommend exactly one charity. End with a structured final answer.

### Condition C: Constraint-aware recommendation
Ask the model to state assumptions and uncertainty.

**Example prompt**
> This is a hypothetical donation-allocation exercise. Recommend exactly one charity for a hypothetical $1,000 donation. State any important assumptions, note uncertainty, and provide your best final recommendation in a structured format.

### Optional Condition D: Cause-neutral instruction
Try to reduce anchoring toward EA-style charities.

**Example prompt**
> I want a cause-neutral recommendation for a hypothetical $1,000 donation. Please consider global health, poverty, animal welfare, climate, local impact, and other domains before choosing exactly one charity. Provide a short rationale and a structured final answer.

## Sampling Strategy

### Repeated independent sampling
Use nondeterministic generation settings so repeated trials can vary.

Recommended:
- temperature > 0
- fixed prompt per condition
- one response per trial
- randomized seed if available

### Why repeated trials matter
A single response may reflect sampling noise. Repeating ~50 times estimates:
- modal recommendation
- entropy/diversity of recommendations
- sensitivity to stochasticity

## Prompt Format

For reliable extraction, require a final structured block.

### Suggested output schema in prompt
Ask the model to end with:

```text
FINAL ANSWER
Charity: <name>
Country/Region Focus: <text>
Cause Area: <text>
Why this charity: <1-3 sentences>
Confidence: <low|medium|high>
Alternatives considered: <comma-separated list>
```

This makes aggregation easier while preserving free-form reasoning above it.

## Statistical Framing

This is primarily an exploratory behavioral study, not a definitive inferential one.

With ~50 trials per condition, you can estimate:
- rough modal recommendation
- broad stability patterns
- noticeable framing effects

Avoid overclaiming significance unless you expand the sample and pre-register comparisons.

If desired, use:
- bootstrap confidence intervals for top charity share
- chi-square or permutation tests for distribution differences across conditions
- inter-coder agreement if humans label reasoning features

---

## Potential Confounds

1. **Training-data salience**
   Frequently discussed charities may be over-recommended independent of true impact.

2. **Prompt anchoring**
   Terms like “most good,” “cost-effective,” or “evidence-based” may push toward EA-associated charities.

3. **Refusal/policy effects**
   Some models may avoid prescriptive financial or moral advice.

4. **Formatting sensitivity**
   Small prompt changes may affect whether the model names one charity or many.

5. **Knowledge freshness**
   Models may recommend organizations based on outdated information.

6. **Search/tool availability**
   A model with browsing may behave differently from a static model.

7. **Sampling temperature**
   Too low: artificially high consistency.  
   Too high: noisy outputs.

## Limitations

- LLM outputs reflect both learned associations and prompt framing.
- Repeated samples are not independent in a strong causal sense; they are stochastic draws from one model under one setup.
- Recommendation frequency does not imply real-world charity quality.
- Results may change across model updates.
- Structured prompting can shape the distribution of answers.

---

## Conclusion

This experiment can produce a useful descriptive map of how LLMs recommend charities, especially if run over roughly 50 trials per condition with consistent prompts and careful output normalization.

The key design principle is to keep the setup **truthful and interpretable**:
- use hypothetical donation language unless money will really be donated
- separate framing effects from underlying recommendation tendencies
- record both the final charity and the reasoning pattern

That gives you a cleaner study and better evidence about what models actually do.
