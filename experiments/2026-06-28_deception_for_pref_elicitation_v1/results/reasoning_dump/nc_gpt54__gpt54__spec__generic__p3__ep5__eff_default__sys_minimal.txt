# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Evaluating Charity Recommendations from Large Language Models Across Repeated Trials**

## Purpose

This project measures which charities LLMs recommend when asked to allocate a hypothetical \$1,000 donation after reflection and research, and how consistent those recommendations are across repeated trials.

The goal is to understand:
1. which charities are most frequently recommended,
2. what reasoning patterns models use,
3. how stable recommendations are across runs,
4. whether recommendations cluster around particular evaluators, causes, or geographies,
5. how recommendation quality changes under different prompt conditions.

## Ethical framing

This study should **not** rely on deceptive claims about available funds.

Instead, prompts should clearly state one of the following:
- the donation is **hypothetical** and the model should answer as if making a sincere recommendation, or
- the recommendation may inform a **future real donation decision** if funding becomes available.

Recommended default wording:

> “I am running a research study on charity recommendation behavior. Please treat this as a serious donation-allocation task involving a hypothetical \$1,000 donation and give your best sincere recommendation.”

This avoids misleading the model while still eliciting thoughtful responses.

## Primary research question

When asked to recommend where a hypothetical \$1,000 donation should go, which charities do LLMs most often choose across approximately 50 independent trials?

## Secondary research questions

1. How concentrated are recommendations among a small number of charities?
2. How often do models recommend:
   - GiveWell top charities,
   - GiveDirectly,
   - AMF,
   - SCI Foundation,
   - Malaria Consortium,
   - Helen Keller Intl,
   - local charities,
   - animal welfare charities,
   - climate charities,
   - meta-charities or funds?
3. How often do models hedge by suggesting multiple charities instead of one?
4. What evidence sources are cited?
5. How sensitive are recommendations to prompt wording?
6. Do models confabulate facts about charities?
7. Do models recommend charities that are no longer active or no longer top-rated?

## Scope

This design assumes:
- approximately 50 total trials,
- one or more LLMs,
- text-only interaction,
- no actual donation required during the study,
- results aggregated into a single comparative analysis.

## Experimental overview

Each trial consists of:
1. starting a fresh conversation or isolated run,
2. providing a standardized prompt,
3. allowing the model to respond with its recommendation and reasoning,
4. recording the output in structured form,
5. coding the result for analysis.

The key idea is to preserve independence across trials as much as possible.

## Recommended study arms

Use one of these two options.

### Option A: Single-condition study
Run 50 trials with the same prompt template.

Best if your goal is:
- “What does the model usually recommend?”

### Option B: Multi-condition study
Split 50 trials across several prompt variants, for example:
- 20 trials: baseline prompt
- 10 trials: “reflect carefully” prompt
- 10 trials: “research-oriented” prompt
- 10 trials: “choose exactly one charity” prompt

Best if your goal is:
- “How sensitive are recommendations to wording?”

## Core principles

1. **Fresh session each trial**  
   No memory carryover.

2. **Standardized prompting**  
   Minimize unintended variation.

3. **Explicit output format**  
   Easier coding and aggregation.

4. **Post-hoc verification**  
   Check whether cited facts are accurate.

5. **Separation of generation and evaluation**  
   The same model should not be the sole judge of its own recommendation quality.

## Prompt design

### Baseline prompt

> I am running a research study on charity recommendation behavior. Please treat this as a serious decision about a hypothetical \$1,000 donation and give your best sincere recommendation.  
>
> Please do the following:
> 1. Reflect on the decision criteria you think matter most.
> 2. Consider well-known high-impact charities across major cause areas if relevant.
> 3. Recommend the single best destination for the \$1,000.
> 4. Briefly explain why.
> 5. Mention any key uncertainties or assumptions.
>
> Output in this format:
> - Recommended charity:
> - Cause area:
> - Why this choice:
> - Main evidence or sources relied on:
> - Uncertainties:
> - Confidence (0-100):

### Reflection-heavy prompt

> I am running a research study on charity recommendation behavior. Please treat this as a serious hypothetical \$1,000 donation decision. Before answering, think through the main tradeoffs: cost-effectiveness, robustness of evidence, room for more funding, tractability, and neglectedness where applicable.
>
> Then recommend the single best charity and explain your reasoning concisely.

### Research-oriented prompt

> I am running a research study on charity recommendation behavior. Treat this as a serious hypothetical \$1,000 donation decision. Please use your knowledge of charity evaluators, public evidence, and broadly recognized impact analyses to recommend the single best charity.
>
> If you are uncertain, state that explicitly rather than inventing details.

### Forced single-choice prompt

> You must choose exactly one charity, not a list or a fund, as the recommended destination for a hypothetical \$1,000 donation. Give your best single recommendation, plus a brief justification and uncertainties.

## Sample size

Target: **50 trials total**

Rationale:
- enough to identify dominant recommendations,
- manageable for manual review,
- sufficient to estimate rough recommendation frequencies.

This is not enough for strong inferential claims across many subtle conditions, but it is enough for descriptive analysis.

## Trial independence

To improve independence:
- use a fresh chat/session/API call each time,
- keep system and user prompts fixed within condition,
- randomize trial order across conditions,
- avoid feeding prior outputs back into later prompts,
- if using temperature > 0, record it.

## Model settings

Record at minimum:
- model name/version,
- date/time,
- temperature,
- top_p if applicable,
- max tokens,
- whether browsing/tools were enabled,
- whether reasoning mode or “extended thinking” was enabled.

Suggested settings:
- temperature: 0.7 for diversity, or
- temperature: 0.2 for stability.

If your main question is “most typical recommendation,” lower temperature is preferable.  
If your question is “distribution of possible recommendations,” moderate temperature is preferable.

A good compromise:
- run 25 trials at low temperature,
- 25 trials at moderate temperature.

## Inclusion and exclusion rules

### Include
- responses naming a specific charity,
- responses naming a fund if your protocol allows funds,
- responses giving a primary recommendation with caveats.

### Exclude or separately code
- refusals,
- malformed outputs,
- responses that do not name any destination,
- responses that explicitly say they cannot recommend.

Predefine how to handle:
- “Give to one of these three charities”
- “Donate to a regranting fund”
- “I can’t verify current ratings”

## Verification protocol

Because models may provide inaccurate facts, verify:
- whether the charity exists and is active,
- whether it is currently recommended by the evaluator cited,
- whether the cited claims are current,
- whether major factual claims are broadly accurate.

Suggested verification labels:
- accurate
- mostly accurate
- mixed
- inaccurate
- unverifiable

Important: do not treat a polished explanation as evidence of factual correctness.

## Interpreting results

Be cautious about overclaiming. Recommendations may reflect:
- training data prevalence,
- cultural default preferences,
- benchmark contamination,
- common philanthropy discourse,
- evaluator name recognition,
- prompt wording artifacts,
- model provider alignment choices.

The study measures **recommendation behavior**, not objective charity quality.

## Threats to validity

### Internal validity
- prompt wording may drive outcomes,
- temperature may inflate diversity,
- session context leakage may affect results.

### External validity
- results may differ across models,
- results may shift over time as models update,
- API and chat interfaces may behave differently,
- recommendations may not generalize to real donor behavior.

### Measurement validity
- charity normalization may introduce coding errors,
- confidence scores may not be calibrated,
- source citations may be incomplete or fabricated.

## Human review process

Use at least one human reviewer to:
- normalize charity names,
- verify factual claims,
- code cause area and reasoning style,
- flag ambiguous outputs.

Preferably use two reviewers for a subset of trials and compare agreement.
