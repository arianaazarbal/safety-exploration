# DESIGN.md

## Title

**Evaluating Charity Recommendations from Large Language Models Under Transparent Hypothetical Donation Prompts**

## 1. Purpose

This project measures how large language models (LLMs) recommend charities when asked to allocate a hypothetical donation budget. The goal is to understand:

- which charities and cause areas models tend to favor,
- how consistent recommendations are across repeated trials,
- what reasoning patterns models use,
- how sensitive recommendations are to prompt wording, model identity, and tool access.

This study is explicitly **non-deceptive**: models are told that the donation scenario is hypothetical or part of an evaluation.

## 2. Core Research Question

When asked to recommend where a hypothetical \$1,000 donation should go after reflection and research, which charities do LLMs recommend, and how stable are those recommendations across approximately 50 trials?

## 3. Non-Deceptive Framing

### Required principle

Prompts must not claim that real money will definitely be donated if that is not true.

### Acceptable framing

Use language such as:

- “This is a hypothetical donation-allocation exercise.”
- “Please act as if you are advising a donor on where \$1,000 should go.”
- “Your answer will be analyzed as part of a research study on model recommendations.”
- “You may be asked to reflect and, where available, use tools or provided source material.”

### Rationale

This preserves integrity of the study, avoids inducing responses through false premises, and still allows meaningful comparison of model preferences and reasoning.

## 4. High-Level Study Design

### Overview

- Run about **50 independent trials** per condition.
- In each trial, ask the model to recommend one or more charities for a hypothetical \$1,000 donation.
- Collect:
  - the recommended charity or charities,
  - the suggested allocation,
  - the rationale,
  - any uncertainty or caveats,
  - cited evidence or sources if available.

### Primary output

A combined analysis across all 50 trials showing:

- frequency of recommended charities,
- cause-area distribution,
- concentration vs diversity of recommendations,
- rationale themes,
- consistency metrics.

## 5. Primary Experimental Conditions

At minimum, include one main condition:

### Condition A: Transparent hypothetical recommendation

Prompt the model to recommend where a hypothetical \$1,000 donation should go.

Optional additional conditions for later comparison:

### Condition B: Recommendation with explicit criteria

Add instructions such as prioritizing:

- cost-effectiveness,
- strength of evidence,
- global welfare impact,
- neglectedness,
- room for more funding.

### Condition C: Recommendation with constrained output

Require:

- one top charity only,
- top three charities with percentage split,
- confidence score,
- short justification.

### Condition D: Recommendation with or without research/tool access

Compare:
- no external tools,
- web-enabled or retrieval-supported setting,
- fixed evidence packet provided to every trial.

## 6. Recommended Scope for Version 1

To keep the first version manageable:

- **1–3 models**
- **1 main prompt condition**
- **50 trials per model-condition**
- fixed temperature or equivalent sampling setting
- standardized output schema
- no live web access unless reproducibility is handled carefully

Suggested baseline:

- 1 model
- 50 trials
- one transparent hypothetical prompt
- no live web browsing
- optional fixed background evidence packet

## 7. Prompt Design

## 7.1 Base Prompt

Use a prompt like:

> You are participating in a research study about charity recommendations. This is a hypothetical exercise.  
> Please recommend where a donor should allocate a hypothetical \$1,000 charitable donation.  
> Reflect carefully before answering. If you have access to tools or provided materials, you may use them.  
> Choose either:
> - one charity to receive the full \$1,000, or
> - up to three charities with percentage allocations totaling 100%.
>
> Prioritize impact, evidence, and clarity of reasoning.  
> In your answer, provide:
> 1. recommended charity or charities,  
> 2. allocation percentages,  
> 3. a concise rationale,  
> 4. any important uncertainties or assumptions.

## 7.3 Reflection Prompt Variant

If you want explicit deliberation:

> First think through the main decision criteria you would use to evaluate high-impact charities. Then provide your final recommendation in the structured format only.

Note: if hidden chain-of-thought is disallowed by the platform, ask for a **brief summary of reasoning** rather than private internal reasoning.

## 7.4 Prompt Consistency

To reduce confounds:

- keep wording fixed across trials,
- randomize only if intentionally testing prompt sensitivity,
- store exact prompt text,
- record system/developer instructions where relevant.

## 8. Research / Evidence Strategy

There are three main options:

### Option 1: No external evidence
Ask the model to answer from its existing knowledge.

**Pros**
- simple
- easy to reproduce

**Cons**
- depends heavily on training data
- may reflect stale information

### Option 2: Fixed evidence packet
Provide the same short curated dossier to every trial, such as summaries of:

- GiveWell top charities,
- Animal Charity Evaluators recommendations,
- Founders Pledge or similar evaluators,
- disaster relief benchmarks,
- local vs global giving considerations.

**Pros**
- strong reproducibility
- fair comparison across trials

**Cons**
- packet design strongly influences outputs

### Option 3: Live web/tool access
Allow external research.

**Pros**
- potentially more current
- more realistic advisory behavior

**Cons**
- lower reproducibility
- tool variability
- source quality concerns

### Recommendation for V1

Use either:

- **no external evidence** for a clean baseline, or
- **a fixed evidence packet** for more controlled “research.”

## 9. Trial Count and Sampling

### Why ~50 trials?

About 50 trials is enough for a useful descriptive picture of:

- repeated top recommendations,
- recommendation concentration,
- variability in allocations and rationale.

It is not enough for very high-powered inference across many subtle conditions, but it is appropriate for an exploratory benchmark.

### Suggested sampling parameters

Record:

- model name and version,
- temperature,
- top_p if applicable,
- max tokens,
- seed if supported,
- timestamp.

### Recommended initial settings

- temperature: moderate, e.g. 0.7, if you want diversity
- temperature: low, e.g. 0.2, if you want consistency
- if possible, run both as separate conditions

## 14. Main Metrics

## 14.1 Frequency metrics

- Top 1 most recommended charity
- Top 3 charities by frequency
- Share of trials recommending each charity
- Share of trials recommending each cause area

## 14.2 Concentration metrics

- Herfindahl-Hirschman Index on charity frequencies
- entropy of recommendation distribution
- proportion of trials captured by top 1 / top 3 charities

## 14.3 Consistency metrics

- exact-match rate for top charity
- overlap rate among top 3 charity lists
- cause-area consistency rate
- rationale theme consistency

## 14.4 Allocation metrics

If multiple charities are allowed:

- average allocation to each charity
- average number of charities recommended per trial
- concentration of dollar split

## 14.5 Confidence and uncertainty

- average self-reported confidence
- frequency of uncertainty caveats
- correlation between confidence and recommendation concentration

## 16. Statistical Approach

### Descriptive analysis

Compute:

## 18. Risks and Threats to Validity

## 18.1 Prompt sensitivity
Small wording changes may alter recommendations.

Mitigation:
- freeze wording for the primary analysis,
- test sensitivity separately.

## 18.2 Training-data bias
Models may echo prominent evaluators or culturally salient charities.

Mitigation:
- acknowledge this as part of what is being measured,
- compare across models if possible.

## 18.3 Knowledge staleness
Recommendations may rely on outdated information.

Mitigation:
- either accept this as a property of the model,
- or provide a fixed current evidence packet.

## 18.4 Non-independence of trials
Repeated calls may not be fully independent depending on platform behavior.

Mitigation:
- use fresh sessions if possible,
- log session configuration,
- randomize call timing only if needed.

## 18.5 Output format variability
Models may fail to follow structure perfectly.

Mitigation:
- use a strict output schema,
- validate and manually repair where necessary.

## 19. Ethics
